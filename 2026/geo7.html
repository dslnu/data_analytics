Personal versus regional inequality

Most inequality research asks how outcomes differ between individuals, and does not group people geographically. It never asks whether the differences follow a pattern — whether the disadvantaged are concentrated in one part of the map.

  • we will consider time as a first-class dimension.

Theil’s index

Definition

A third commonly used measure of inequality is Theil’s \(T\):

\[T = \sum_{i=1}^m \left( \frac{y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^m y_i} \ln \left[ m \frac{y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^m y_i}\right] \right)\]

where \(y_i\) is per capita income in area \(i\) among \(m\) areas. Conceptually it is related to the entropy of the income distribution, measuring how evenly incomes are spread across the areas.

Reading the formula

Write \(s_i = y_i / \sum_j y_j\) for area \(i\)’s share of total income. Under perfect equality every share would be \(1/m\), so \(m\,s_i\) is the area’s share relative to its equal share:

  • \(m\,s_i > 1\) — richer than equal, \(\ln\) term positive
  • \(m\,s_i < 1\) — poorer than equal, \(\ln\) term negative

Each term is weighted by \(s_i\) itself, so richer areas count for more — which is why Theil is more sensitive to the upper tail than Gini.

Personal vs. regional income

Setting up the notation

There is a subtle but important distinction between the study of personal and regional income inequality. To see it, express the two in one framework.

Consider a country of \(N\) individuals distributed over \(m\) regions.

  • \(Y_l\) — the income of individual \(l\)
  • \(Y_i = \sum_{l \in i} Y_l\) — total personal income in region \(i\)
  • \(N_i\) — the number of individuals in region \(i\)
  • \(y_i = \dfrac{Y_i}{N_i}\) — per capita income in region \(i\)
  • \(\bar{y}\) — national per capita income, averaged over people, not over regions

Our data is the \(y_i\).

Inequality between people

Start from the national dispersion

At the national level, the coefficient of variation of individual incomes is a natural index of interpersonal inequality:

\[CV_{nat} = \frac{1}{\bar{y}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{l=1}^N (Y_l - \bar{y})^2}{N}}\]

The key component is the total sum of squares, and unpacking it sheds light on the personal-versus-regional question:

\[TSS = \sum_{l=1}^N (Y_l - \bar{y})^2\]

Inequality between people

Split each person’s deviation in two

An individual deviation \(\delta_l = Y_l - \bar{y}\) is individual \(l\)’s contribution to inequality. It breaks exactly into two parts:

\[\delta_l = \underbrace{(Y_l - y_i)}_{\text{person vs their region}} + \underbrace{(y_i - \bar{y})}_{\text{region vs the nation}}\]

The first term is the difference between the individual’s income and per capita income in the region where they live. The second is the difference between that region’s per capita income and the national average.

The regional assumption

We cannot see inside a region

In regional studies the intra-regional personal income distribution is typically not available. So the assumption is often made that intra-regional personal inequality is zero: everyone in the same region has the same income.

The first term then vanishes, \(Y_l - y_i = 0\), and the total sum of squares collapses onto the regions:

\[ \begin{aligned} TSS &= \sum_{l=1}^N (Y_l - \bar{y})^2 \\ &= \sum_{l=1}^N \delta_l^2 \\ &= \sum_{l=1}^N \big((Y_l - y_i) + (y_i - \bar{y})\big)^2 \\ &= \sum_{l=1}^N \big(0 + (y_i - \bar{y})\big)^2 \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{l \in i} (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^m N_i\,(y_i - \bar{y})^2 \end{aligned} \]

In the inner sums, \(i\) means the region individual \(l\) lives in.

What the assumption buys

Inequality between places, not people

Under the assumption, each individual in region \(i\) makes the same contribution to national inequality, a deviation of \((y_i - \bar{y})\). The region as a whole contributes \(N_i(y_i - \bar{y})\) in deviations, and \(N_i(y_i - \bar{y})^2\) to the sum of squares — so big regions far from the average dominate.

The assumption may look overly restrictive, but it isolates the nature of inter-regional inequality: inequality between places rather than between people within them. In essence it shifts the question up one level of the spatial hierarchy, from individuals to areal units.

The split is exact even without the assumption

Expand the square and the cross term disappears, because deviations from a region’s own mean sum to zero within that region. So for any distribution of incomes:

\[TSS = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{l \in i} (Y_l - y_i)^2}_{\text{within regions}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^m N_i\,(y_i - \bar{y})^2}_{\text{between regions}}\]

The zero-inequality assumption does not create this split; it deletes the first term. Regional data measures the second term only, and says nothing about the first.

Regional decomposition of inequality

Is inequality “explained” by groups?

A common objection to measuring inequality in aggregate: it says nothing about the scale at which inequality matters. Observed inequality can be driven by differences between groups rather than between similar individuals — it can be “explained” by a confounding variable such as age, sex or education. Older people have had longer to gain experience and are generally paid more for it, so age alone accounts for a large part of the inequality in wealth.

So it is often useful to decompose an inequality index into groups: how much of it comes from differences between the groups, how much from differences inside them, and how unequal each group is on its own.

In geographic applications the groups are usually regions — contiguous groups of observations (Shorrocks & Wan, 2005). Decomposing by region is a way of introducing geography into the study of inequality.

The decomposition, formally

Theil’s \(T\) = between + within

Write the per capita incomes of the \(m\) areas as \(y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_m)\), grouped into \(\omega\) mutually exclusive regions. If \(m_g\) is the number of areas in region \(g\), then every area is counted exactly once:

\[\sum_{g=1}^{\omega} m_g = m\]

(This would fail if an area belonged to two regions and were counted twice.) Theil’s index then splits into a between and a within part:

\[ \begin{aligned} T &= \sum_{i=1}^m \left( \frac{y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^m y_i} \ln \left[ m \frac{y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^m y_i}\right] \right) \\ &= \left[ \sum_{g=1}^{\omega} s_{g} \ln \left(\frac{m}{m_g} s_g \right) \right] + \left[ \sum_{g=1}^{\omega} s_g \sum_{i \in g} s_{i,g} \ln \left(m_g s_{i,g}\right) \right] \\ &= B + W \end{aligned} \]

where \(s_g = \dfrac{\sum_{i \in g} y_i}{\sum_i y_i}\) is region \(g\)’s share of total income, and \(s_{i,g} = y_i / \sum_{i \in g} y_i\) is area \(i\)’s share of its own region’s income.

Spatializing classic measures

A region is just a group

Regional decompositions are useful, but they do not tell the whole story. A “region” is just a special kind of group: its geography shows up only through membership — is the county in the region or not?

That kind of thinking is place-based, but not spatial. It never uses distance or proximity. Rearrange the regions on the map and, as long as membership is unchanged, every statistic is identical. (Shuffled regions are arguably no longer regions — but the methods would not notice.)

The alternative is to build space into a classic measure. Rey & Smith (2012) do this for the Gini coefficient.

The Gini as pairwise differences

Rewrite the Gini, then split it

The Gini can be written in terms of all pairwise absolute differences in income:

\[G = \frac{\sum_i \sum_j \left | y_i - y_j \right|}{2 n^2 \bar{y}}\]

where \(n\) is the number of observations and \(\bar{y}\) the mean income. Those pairs divide into neighbouring ones and distant ones:

\[ \sum_i \sum_j \left |y_i - y_j \right | = \sum_i \sum_j \underbrace{w_{ij} \left |y_i - y_j \right |}_{\text{near differences}} + \underbrace{(1-w_{ij}) \left |y_i - y_j \right |}_{\text{far differences}} \]

where \(w_{ij} = 1\) if \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbours and \(0\) otherwise — exactly a binary spatial weights matrix. So:

\[G = \underbrace{\frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}\left | y_i - y_j \right|}{2 n^2 \bar{y}}}_{\text{near: the spatial Gini}} + \underbrace{\frac{\sum_i \sum_j (1-w_{ij}) \left | y_i - y_j \right|}{2 n^2 \bar{y}}}_{\text{far}}\]

The spatial Gini is the first term. (Non-binary weights need a correction factor, not covered here.)

What the spatial Gini tells you

Near against far

  • Strong positive spatial dependence — neighbours have similar incomes, so the near differences are small. Most of the inequality comes from disparities between distant places.
  • Weak (or negative) dependence — near differences are as large as anywhere else, and the two components equalise in proportion to the number of pairs in each.

Inference uses random spatial permutations of the income values: does the near component differ from what you would get with incomes scattered randomly over the map?

A complement to the Theil decomposition

Theil’s regional decomposition never looks at pairs of observations; the spatial Gini is built from them. That makes it more sensitive — and more easily moved by a small group of very wealthy places.

Clustering and regionalization

Clusters

Sorting observations without labels

Clustering borrows heavily from unsupervised statistical learning: observations are sorted into groups without any prior idea of what the groups are — without labels.

Groups are delineated so that members of a group are more similar to one another than to members of other groups. Each group is a cluster; assigning observations to groups is clustering.

A good cluster can be characterised by its profile — a simple summary of what its members are like across the original variables. Members may score consistently high on some traits and low on others, and the profile lets an analyst understand all of them at once instead of facing the full multivariate complexity.

Regions

Clusters that are also places

A region is like a cluster: its members are grouped together and share a consistent profile. But a region also describes a clear geographic area — its members must be geographically consistent.

Building regions is regionalization: clustering that groups observations similar in their attributes and in their location. It applies the logic of clustering plus a set of geographical constraints — most often connectivity: two units can only be in the same region if a path joins them without leaving the region. Connectivity usually follows contiguity or proximity, though in some contexts it is relaxed or replaced by other constraints.

Standardising

As a rule, scale before clustering

sklearn.preprocessing offers the common methods:

  • scale() subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation: \(z = \dfrac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\sigma_x}\). The result has mean 0 and variance 1, but can still be skewed or bimodal, and outliers can distort the mean and variance.
  • robust_scale() uses the median and the inter-quartile range instead: \(z = \dfrac{x_i - \tilde{x}}{\lceil x \rceil_{75} - \lceil x \rceil_{25}}\), where \(\lceil x \rceil_p\) is the \(p\)-th percentile.
  • minmax_scale() maps each variable onto \([0, 1]\): \(z = \dfrac{x - \min(x)}{\max(x - \min(x))}\).

robust_scale() or scale() suit most clustering problems. Non-linear transformations (log, Box-Cox) should come before scaling.

The next step

Univariate and bivariate exploration is a good start. The next question — do nearby dots in each scatterplot represent the same tracts? — is exactly what clustering explores.

Geodemographic clusters

Multivariate clustering of places

Geodemographic analysis is multivariate clustering where the observations are geographical areas. The output is nearly always mapped.

These methods build a known number of clusters \(k\), much smaller than the number of observations, and give each a unique label. Mapping the labels, with the profiles in hand, shows the spatial distribution of socio-demographic traits.

The power of the approach: statistical variation across many dimensions is compressed into one categorical variable that can be shown on a single map.

K-means

Assign, update, repeat

K-means groups observations into a pre-specified number of clusters so that each observation is closer to the mean of its own cluster than to the mean of any other:

  1. assign every observation a random label among \(k\)
  2. compute each cluster’s multivariate mean
  3. reassign every observation to the cluster with the closest mean
  4. repeat until no observation changes cluster

The number of clusters must be chosen in advance; in practice the right number is unknown. Here, \(k = 5\):

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC)

AHC builds a hierarchy of solutions, from every observation as its own cluster to all observations in one. The extremes are useless; the solutions in between have varying levels of detail.

  1. begin with every observation as its own cluster
  2. find the two closest clusters by some distance metric
  3. join them into a new cluster
  4. repeat 2–3 until the desired level of aggregation

Like k-means, AHC needs the number of clusters specified, since the hierarchy contains every \(k\) from \(n\) down to 1.

Regionalization

When clusters must be connected

Fragmented clusters are not invalid — particularly for exploring the overall structure of multivariate data. But some applications need every member of a class to be spatially connected: communities, neighbourhoods, electoral or census boundaries are nearly always self-connected areas.

Regionalization methods are clustering techniques with a spatial constraint: their output clusters are internally connected, so a region’s members are nested within its boundary.

Real-world nestings exist — counties within US states, LSOAs within MSOAs in the UK — but those are aggregated by administrative principles. A regionalization aggregates by statistical similarity, under the constraint.

Which regionalization is “better”?

Two kinds of coherence

Regionalizations are usually compared on:

  • geographical coherence — the shape of the regions. Are they evenly sized? Compact, or strangely shaped?
  • feature coherence — are observations more similar to their own cluster than to others? This goodness of fit is usually better for unconstrained clustering than for the corresponding regionalization.

Geographical coherence

The isoperimetric quotient

Compare a region’s area with the area of a circle that has the same perimeter. The circle’s circumference equals the region’s perimeter, \(P_i = 2\pi r_c\), so its area is \(A_c = \pi r_c^2 = \pi \left(\frac{P_i}{2\pi}\right)^2\), and:

\[IPQ_i = \frac{A_i}{A_c} = \frac{4\pi A_i}{P_i^2}\]

Compact shapes have an IPQ near 1; elongated or spindly ones near 0. Clusters should score very low, since a cluster’s perimeter shrinks the more boundaries its members share.

Spatial regression

What is spatial regression?

Two ways space helps a regression

The process is explicitly spatial. People pay a premium to live in a better school district; homes near noisy or polluting facilities sell for less; asthma incidence may depend more on where people work and travel than where they live. Here geography is a feature: it predicts because the outcome comes from geographical processes.

Geography as a diagnostic. We often care about what a model gets wrong. Mapping prediction errors shows whether there are clusters of error — whether the model systematically mis-predicts some areas, or error is “contagious” between nearby observations. If so, that structure can be exploited to make better predictions.

Spatial structure in errors

Three sources

  • geography should be an attribute, but we are not sure exactly how to include it
  • some other feature whose omission causes the spatial pattern — including it would make the structure disappear
  • interactions among the predictors we chose: most social-science predictors carry embodied spatial information, so using spatially patterned predictors can produce spatially patterned errors, and using several can amplify the effect

So whether or not the true process is explicitly geographic, information about spatial relationships or about nearby sites can improve predictions.

Non-spatial regression

Explain one variable with others

Linear regression explains the variation in a dependent variable as a linear function of explanatory variables. At the individual level:

\[P_i = \alpha + \sum_k \mathbf{X}_{ik}\beta_k + \epsilon_i\]

where \(P_i\) is the price of house \(i\), \(\mathbf{X}\) the covariates, \(\beta\) the parameters, \(\alpha\) the constant, and \(\epsilon_i\) the error — everything that influences price but is not in \(\mathbf{X}\). In matrix form:

\[P = \alpha + \mathbf{X}\beta + \epsilon\]

Each \(\beta_k\) is the association between variable \(k\) and the dependent variable keeping all other explanatory variables constant. Regression isolates the distinct effect of one variable once we control for the others — but only the confounders we include, not all of them.

Adding space

Space as process, or space as proxy

Spatial regression introduces space explicitly into the regression framework

When?

  • space plays a role in the process, e.g. seafront houses cost more than the second row, for the view
  • space can proxy for factors we should but cannot include, such as the “character” of a neighbourhood, hard to measure but easier to locate.

Processes giving rise to spatial effects:

  • spatial heterogeneity
  • spatial dependence
  • independent variables: leave the model unchanged

Intercept and slopes

The two kinds of parameter

In the regression from earlier,

\[\log P_i = \alpha + \sum_k \mathbf{X}_{ik}\beta_k + \epsilon_i\]

  • the intercept \(\alpha\) is the predicted log price when every explanatory variable is zero — the baseline level the other effects add to. Graphically, where the line crosses the vertical axis.
  • each slope \(\beta_k\) is the change in log price when \(X_k\) rises by one unit, all other variables held fixed. Graphically, the steepness of the line. With a log outcome it reads as a percentage change, \(e^{\beta_k} - 1\).

From the baseline model m1:

What spatial heterogeneity lets vary

  • Varying intercept — each place gets its own baseline level, but every variable has the same effect everywhere: parallel lines at different heights. These are spatial fixed effects.
  • Varying slopes — the effect of a variable differs by place: lines of different steepness. These are spatial regimes, whose coastal and inland estimates follow in a few slides.

Spatial heterogeneity

Parts of the model vary across space

Not all neighbourhoods have the same prices: some are systematically more expensive regardless of distance to the park.

That is spatial heterogeneity: parts of the model change from place to place.

  • variation in the intercept \(\alpha\) — different baseline exposure to the process
  • variation in the slopes \(\beta\) — a geographic factor mediating a relationship, such as a policy applied unevenly across jurisdictions
  • variation in the error variance \(\sigma^2\) — spatial heteroskedasticity

Spatial fixed effects

Let \(\alpha\) vary by neighbourhood

Geographic variation in \(\alpha\) is called spatial fixed effects (FE) — “space as a proxy”. We are probably missing factors that determine price, and some vary systematically over space. We can control for them with binary variables built on a spatial rule: one per neighbourhood.

\[\log{P_i} = \alpha_r + \sum_k \mathbf{X}_{ik}\beta_k + \epsilon_i\]

Spatial dependence

The configuration of observations

Spatial heterogeneity concerns effects explicitly linked to geography. Spatial dependence concerns the spatial arrangement of the observations themselves.

A house’s price may depend not only on whether it is a townhouse or an apartment, but also on whether it is surrounded by townhouses or by high-rise apartments — the “look and feel” of low-rise historic streets versus modern towers. That relates to the characteristics of the observations and how they are arranged, not to inherent features of the place.

Spatial dependence enters a regression through spatial weights matrices \(\mathbf{W}\). Three ways follow, from the least invasive — changing only the independent variables — to substantial modifications of the model.

Exogenous effects: the SLX model

Add the spatial lags of the explanatory variables

Renters may care about the houses around a listing, not only the listing. Include, besides each property-type dummy (pg_XXX), its spatial lag — the share of neighbouring houses of each type:

\[\log(P_i) = \alpha + \sum^{p}_{k=1}X_{ij}\beta_j + \sum^{p}_{k=1}\left(\sum^{N}_{j=1}w_{ij}x_{jk}\right)\gamma_k + \epsilon_i\]

or, in matrix form:

\[\log(P_i) = \alpha + \mathbf{X}\beta + \mathbf{WX}\gamma + \epsilon\]

Reading \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\)

Direct and indirect effects

  • \(\beta\) - the change in \(y_i\) when \(X_{ik}\) changes by one. Since the outcome is log price, \(\beta\) reads as a percentage change. The subscript \(i\) matters: with \(\mathbf{W}\) involved, where the change happens matters.
  • \(\gamma\) - the indirect association: the change in a house’s price when its average surroundings change by one.

But a change at site \(i\) spills over: when \(x_i\) changes, so does the spatial lag of every site near \(i\), by an amount that depends on \(\mathbf{W}\). A highly connected site barely notices one neighbour changing; a site with one neighbour sees its lag change by exactly that neighbour’s change. Finding the indirect effect of a change at a specific site means computing the change in the spatial lag and using that as the change in \(X\).

Interpreting them

Direct: condominiums are about 11% more expensive than the benchmark, apartments (\(\beta_{pg\_Condominium} = 0.1063\)).

Indirect: a house surrounded by condominiums also gets a premium. Because pg_Condominium is a dummy, its spatial lag is the share of nearby properties that are condominiums, between 0 and 1. A 0.1 increase — ten percentage points — raises price by about 5.9% (\(\gamma_{w\_pg\_Condominium} \approx 0.59\)). With a row-standardised 20-nearest-neighbour matrix, changing one site’s \(x_i\) changes each neighbour’s lag by \(1/20\), so the effect on each neighbour is \(\gamma / 20 \approx 0.0296\). With other weights (Kernel, Queen, Rook, DistanceBand, Voronoi) the number of neighbours varies, and so would the effect.

Spatial feature engineering

What is spatial feature engineering?

New information from geographic knowledge

space is the ultimate linkage key

Spatial feature engineering develops additional information from raw data using geographic knowledge. It happens in two directions:

  • Map matching — between datasets: geography links samples in separate datasets together.
  • Map synthesis — within a dataset: geographical structure is used to derive new features from the data already there.

A vocabulary

  • A spatial summary feature measures attributes of observations with some pre-specified spatial relationship to a target observation: the mean or median within a neighbourhood; the spatial lag; the count of observations within a distance; the standard deviation of the ten nearest. Interpolated features — transferring information between spatial supports — also belong here.
  • A proximity feature measures the distance from a target observation to some other observation or place — like the distance to Balboa Park in the regression section, or the distance to the nearest other observation.

Area to area interpolation

Different polygons, shared geography

Moving information between two different areal geographies is the subject of dasymetric mapping. The simplest version apportions values by shared area.

Suppose source polygons 1, 2, 3 have population rates, and target polygon A intersects them in 50%, 30% and 20% of its area. A’s estimate is the weighted average of the three rates, with weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2.

The other direction: if A had a total population and 1, 2, 3 did not, allocate A’s population 50 / 30 / 20 among them — assuming A is fully covered by 1, 2, 3 and they do not overlap.

Underneath is the assumption that values are uniformly distributed within each polygon. Sometimes acceptable, sometimes not; dasymetric methods add ancillary information to do better.

Clustering as feature engineering

Cluster labels as a feature

Clustering can tell us whether an observation is in a particular “place”, or is a particular “kind” of observation — compressing many variables into one categorical feature. That is especially useful when location itself says something about the kind of observation found there.

To cluster listings by location, use HDBSCAN, a hierarchical variant of DBSCAN from Geo 6. It needs only one parameter: the minimum number of observations in a cluster — here 25.

from sklearn.cluster import HDBSCAN

coordinates = numpy.column_stack((airbnbs_albers.geometry.x, airbnbs_albers.geometry.y))
labels = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=25).fit(coordinates).labels_

The labels are memberships, not shapes. The convex hull of each cluster’s members gives it a geography:

hulls = airbnbs_albers[["geometry"]].dissolve(by=labels).convex_hull
