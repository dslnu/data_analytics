Spatial inequality and its dynamics

Two literatures that rarely meet

Personal versus regional inequality

Most inequality research asks how outcomes differ between individuals, and does not group people geographically. It never asks whether the differences follow a pattern — whether the disadvantaged are concentrated in one part of the map.

While the two literatures (personal and regional inequality) are related, they have developed in a largely parallel fashion with limited cross-fertilization.

This section takes the regional view, and it is also the only place in the course where time is a first-class dimension. Everything else has been a snapshot.

Global inequality: the 20:20 ratio

The simplest robust measure

The income at the 80th percentile divided by the income at the 20th:

r2020 = [pci[y].quantile(0.8) / pci[y].quantile(0.2) for y in years]

In 1969 the richest 20% of counties had incomes 1.5× the poorest 20%. Its virtue is robustness: it ignores both tails entirely, so a handful of extreme counties cannot move it.

Its vice is the same thing — it uses two numbers and discards the rest of the distribution.

The Lorenz curve

Cumulative share against cumulative population

Plot the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of population, both sorted poorest-first. Perfect equality is the diagonal: \(p\)% of the population earning exactly \(p\)% of the income.

The Gini index is the area between the diagonal and the curve, divided by the area under the diagonal (\(\tfrac12\)). It summarises the whole curve in one number.

They disagree

Gini fell. Theil rose.

Over the same 49 years, on the same data:

1969 2017 direction
Gini 0.1356 0.1284 down
Theil 0.0296 0.0310 up

Neither is wrong. They weight the distribution differently — Gini is most sensitive around the middle, Theil (a generalised entropy measure) gives more weight to the upper tail. A distribution that compresses in the middle while stretching at the top moves them in opposite directions.

“Did inequality rise?” is not answerable without saying which inequality, and reporting a single index without saying why you chose it hides the decision.

Personal versus regional, formally

Why the distinction matters

With \(N\) individuals across \(m\) regions, an individual’s deviation from the national mean decomposes exactly:

\[\delta_l = \underbrace{(Y_l - y_i)}_{\text{within their region}} + \underbrace{(y_i - \bar{y})}_{\text{their region vs the nation}}\]

So national interpersonal inequality contains a regional component and a personal-within-region component. Our county data only sees the second term’s geography — each county is already an average.

This is the ecological problem from G1 in a different guise: county-level inequality is not person-level inequality, and a fall in one is compatible with a rise in the other.

Regional decomposition

Theil splits exactly; Gini does not

The Theil index belongs to the generalised entropy family, the only class that decomposes additively into between-group and within-group parts:

\[T = T_{\text{between}} + T_{\text{within}}\]

from inequality.theil import TheilD

td = TheilD(pci[years].values, pci.Region.values)

for lbl, i in [("1969", 0), ("2017", -1)]:
    b, w_ = td.bg[i], td.wg[i]
    print(f"  {lbl}:  between {b:.4f}   within {w_:.4f}   between-share {b / (b + w_):.1%}")
  1969:  between 0.0091   within 0.0204   between-share 30.9%
  2017:  between 0.0053   within 0.0257   between-share 17.1%

The US Census Bureau’s eight regions supply the grouping.

What the decomposition reveals

f, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.4, 3))

The aggregate was hiding two opposite trends

  • Between-region inequality fell — 0.0091 → 0.0053. American regions converged.
  • Within-region inequality rose — 0.0204 → 0.0257. Counties inside each region pulled apart.
  • The between-share collapsed from 30.9% to 17.1%.

The total barely moved because the components moved in opposite directions and roughly cancelled. Reporting only the total would have described 49 years of substantial structural change as “not much happened”.

Decomposition depends on your regions

MAUP, now inside a statistic

The between/within split is a property of the grouping you chose. Use states instead of census regions and the between-share rises, because states are smaller and more homogeneous internally.

There is no correct grouping, so “most inequality is within regions” is a statement about census regions as much as about the economy. Report the grouping alongside the number, and ideally show the split under two or three alternatives.

This is the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem from G1, arriving in an index rather than a map.

Inequality with a geography

Is high income clustered, and is it becoming more so?

Neither Gini nor Theil knows where anything is — shuffle the counties across the map and both are unchanged. To bring geography in, return to Moran’s \(I\) from Geo 6 and compute it for every year.

from esda.moran import Moran

wq = W.Queen.from_dataframe(pci, use_index=False)
wq.transform = "r"
morans = [Moran(pci[y].values, wq).I for y in years]

for lbl, i in [("1969", 0), ("1993", 24), ("2017", -1)]:
    print(f"  {lbl} : Moran's I = {morans[i]:.4f}")

  1993 : Moran's I = 0.5090
  2017 : Moran's I = 0.4212
Code

Reading that trend

Spatial clustering of income has weakened steadily

\(I\) falls from 0.649 to 0.421 — still strongly positive, but a clear monotone decline.

Rich counties are still next to rich counties. But the map has become less organised by geography over fifty years: where you are predicts your income less well in 2017 than in 1969.

Note this is a different question from whether inequality rose. Income could become more unequal and less spatially clustered at the same time — and in this data, roughly that is what happened.

And a third construction

inequality.gini.Gini_Spatial goes further, splitting the Gini itself into contributions from neighbouring versus non-neighbouring county pairs — putting space inside the index rather than measuring it alongside.

Clustering and regionalization

Geodemographics

Compressing many variables into one label

A census tract carries dozens of variables. Geodemographic analysis finds groups of tracts that are similar across all of them at once, and gives each group a name — “affluent suburban”, “young urban renters”.

It is unsupervised: there are no labels, so nothing tells you the answer is right. What you get is a typology, and its value is whether it is useful and interpretable, not whether it is correct.

The result is used constantly — in retail site selection, public health targeting, political campaigning and resource allocation.

Scale before you cluster

Look at the ranges above. median_house_value runs into the hundreds of thousands; pct_white is bounded by 100. Euclidean distance is dominated by whichever variable has the largest numbers, so without scaling the clustering is essentially univariate.

Hierarchical clustering

A different algorithm on identical data

ARI(k-means, ward) = 0.5821

Ward’s linkage merges the pair of clusters that increases within-cluster variance least — the agglomerative analogue of what k-means optimises.

Two defensible answers

ARI 0.58 is moderate agreement, not agreement

Same data, same number of clusters, two standard algorithms, and they agree only moderately. Roughly, the big structure is shared and the boundaries are not.

There is no label to check against, so neither is right. What you can do:

  • report that the partition is algorithm-dependent, rather than presenting one
  • check stability by subsampling, as in Bio 4
  • choose on interpretability — which typology can you describe to a domain expert?

This is the same situation as Leiden resolution in Bio 4: a defensible choice masquerading as a computation.

The spatial problem

Count the pieces

Neither algorithm knows about geography, so a “cluster” can be scattered across the county. Dissolve each cluster and count its connected components:

def pieces(frame, col):
    diss = frame.dissolve(by=col)
    return [len(g.geoms) if g.geom_type == "MultiPolygon" else 1 for g in diss.geometry]

pw = pieces(sdt, "ward5")
print("ward  : pieces per cluster", pw, "-> total", sum(pw))
ward  : pieces per cluster [24, 5, 10, 17, 15] -> total 71

Five clusters, 71 disconnected fragments. As a typology that is fine — “affluent suburban” describes tracts anywhere. As regions it is useless: you cannot administer, fund or police a territory in 71 pieces.

Regionalization

Clustering with a contiguity constraint

Pass the spatial weights matrix as a connectivity graph and the algorithm may only merge clusters that are geographically adjacent.

from libpysal.weights import Queen

wq_sd = Queen.from_dataframe(sdt, use_index=False)
region = AgglomerativeClustering(
    linkage="ward", connectivity=wq_sd.sparse, n_clusters=5
).fit(X)
sdt["reg5"] = region.labels_

pr = pieces(sdt, "reg5")
print("regions: pieces per cluster", pr, "-> total", sum(pr))
print("sizes  :", numpy.bincount(region.labels_).tolist())
regions: pieces per cluster [1, 1, 1, 2, 2] -> total 7
sizes  : [275, 85, 1, 227, 40]

From 71 pieces to 7. The constraint does exactly what it promised.

What the constraint costs

Geographic coherence is bought with feature coherence

print(f"ARI(ward, regionalized) = {metrics.adjusted_rand_score(ward.labels_, region.labels_):.4f}")

for name, lab in [("ward", ward.labels_), ("regionalized", region.labels_)]:
    print(f"  {name:<13} silhouette on features = {metrics.silhouette_score(X, lab):.4f}")
ARI(ward, regionalized) = 0.2474
  ward          silhouette on features = 0.1623
  regionalized  silhouette on features = 0.0615

The partitions are substantially different, and the regionalized one fits the features less well — necessarily, since it is optimising the same objective under an extra constraint.

That is the trade-off in one number. There is no free contiguity.

And watch the sizes

The constrained solution here produces a cluster of one tract. Contiguity-constrained agglomeration can strand an isolated unit that is adjacent only to very different neighbours. Check cluster sizes before interpreting; a singleton “region” is usually a boundary artefact, not a finding.

How to judge a regionalisation

Three criteria, and they conflict

  • Geographical coherence — are the regions contiguous and compact? Count the pieces, as above.
  • Feature coherence — how well do members resemble each other? Silhouette, or within-cluster variance.
  • Solution similarity — how much does the answer move under a different algorithm, seed or \(k\)? ARI between solutions.

You cannot maximise all three. Which matters depends on what the regions are for: a typology for marketing wants feature coherence, a set of administrative districts needs geographic coherence, and a finding you intend to publish needs stability.

Why geography belongs in a model

Two arguments

One enthusiastic, one reluctant

The process is genuinely spatial. People pay a premium to live in a better school district; homes near a motorway or a waste plant sell for less; asthma incidence depends on where you spend your day, not where you sleep. Here geography is a feature — it predicts because the outcome was produced geographically.

Or: geography is a useful diagnostic. Even if you do not believe the process is spatial, mapping your errors tells you whether the model fails systematically somewhere. If error is “contagious” between nearby observations, that structure is exploitable.

Regardless of whether or not the true process is explicitly geographic, additional information about the spatial relationships between our observations can make our predictions better.

Where spatial error comes from

Three sources, and none of them require a “spatial process”

  • Geography should have been an attribute and you did not know how to include it.
  • An omitted variable is spatially patterned. Its absence shows up as structure in the residuals — a spatially patterned omitted covariate.
  • Your predictors carry embodied spatial information. Most social-science variables are spatially patterned for free. Use one and you may get patterned errors; use several and the effect compounds.

This is why spatial diagnostics are worth running on models you did not think were spatial.

The specification

Ten covariates, no geography

\[P_i = \alpha + \sum_k \mathbf{X}_{ik}\beta_k + \epsilon_i\]

variable_names = [
    "accommodates", "bathrooms", "bedrooms", "beds",
    "rt_Private_room", "rt_Shared_room",
    "pg_Condominium", "pg_House", "pg_Other", "pg_Townhouse",
]

We model log_price rather than price, so coefficients read as approximate percentage changes.

Each \(\beta_k\) is the association between that variable and price holding the others constant. Regression is not magic: it discounts confounders you included, not all confounders.

A caveat the book flags

Hidden structure in the errors

The i.i.d. assumption

What linear models assume, and what geography does to it

OLS assumes errors are independent and identically distributed. If residuals cluster in space, that assumption is violated — and the standard errors, \(t\)-statistics and \(p\)-values that follow from it are wrong.

So the question is not academic. Before trusting any coefficient in the table above, we should ask: do the errors have a geography?

Three checks, in increasing sharpness:

  1. split the residuals by a known spatial category
  2. look at their distribution by neighbourhood
  3. correlate each residual with the residuals around it

Check 1 — coastal or not

A theory: the model knows nothing about beaches

San Diego is a coastal city and the model has no beach variable. If proximity to the ocean matters, coastal listings should be systematically under-predicted.

A residual mean that differs by a known spatial category is a model failure, not a curiosity. It says the model is systematically wrong in a place you can name.

Check 3 — the sharpest one

Correlate each error with its neighbour’s error

If pricing is contagious — hosts price against nearby listings — errors should be too. Take the single nearest listing to each property and compare residuals.

Why this matters

If mis-prediction has a consistent geography, we can model it. The rest of the lecture is three different ways of doing exactly that.

Space as a feature

Spatial feature engineering

Two directions

At its core, spatial feature engineering is the process of developing additional information from raw data using geographic knowledge.

Map matching between datasets geography is the join key
Map synthesis within one dataset geography borrows from nearby rows

Space is the ultimate linkage key. Two datasets with no shared identifier — Airbnb listings and census tracts, say — can still be joined, because both are somewhere. That is a trick you cannot pull with aspatial data.

A vocabulary

Two families of engineered feature

Spatial summary features measure attributes of observations bearing some spatial relationship to the target:

  • the mean or median of features within a neighbourhood
  • the spatial lag — the average of the neighbours’ values
  • counts within a distance, standard deviation of the ten nearest
  • interpolated features, transferring information between supports

Proximity features measure distance from the target to something else — a park, the coast, the nearest other observation.

Both can be built by matching or by synthesis.

The precision trade-off

More accurate transfer costs more

There is always an inevitable trade-off between precision and sophistication: more precise transfer is usually possible at the expense of more advanced and involved techniques.

That cost is both computational and cognitive — someone has to understand the method later. Sometimes you need the best possible estimate; often “good enough” is good enough, and the simple spatial join wins.

Reproject the points to the raster’s CRS, not the other way round. Resampling a raster is lossy and slow; transforming a few thousand points is neither.

dem.sample() does nearest-cell lookup. A smoother estimate would interpolate between neighbouring cells — the precision/sophistication trade-off again, at its smallest scale.

When there is no surface

Spatial interpolation

Sometimes you have measurements at points that are not the points you care about, and no ready-made surface. Then you must guess the field between them.

The serious methods are kriging (Gaussian process regression, from geostatistics) and geographically weighted regression. The book uses something deliberately simpler:

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor

model = KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=10, weights="distance")
model.fit(known_locations, known_values)
surface = model.predict(grid_locations)

Ten nearest listings, distance-weighted. Not sophisticated — but it makes the point that interpolation is just prediction where the only features are coordinates. Remember that; it comes back in part 5.

To attach census tract population density to each listing, the geographic problem is only “which polygon contains this point?” — a spatial join.

The hard case

Where did 60% of the population go?

Read the totals, always

The interpolated total is far below the source total. That is not a bug, and working out why is the whole lesson.

print(f"census extent : {census.area.sum() / 1e6:>8,.0f} km2")
print(f"hex grid      : {hexes.area.sum() / 1e6:>8,.0f} km2")

hex grid      :      724 km2
grid covers   :     4.3% of the census area
but captures  :    40.0% of the population

The hex grid covers only the urban core. Mass is preserved only where the target covers the source — everything outside the grid is silently dropped.

Note the ratio, though: 4% of the land holds 40% of the people. Which is also the reason simple areal weighting is crude — it assumes population is uniform within each source tract, and population is never uniform. Dasymetric methods use ancillary data (land cover, building footprints, night lights) to distribute it more honestly.

The habit worth forming

After any areal interpolation, compare the source and target totals. If they differ, you must be able to say why before you use the result.

The most useful engineered variable here

The spatial lag of a feature answers “what is around me?” without needing any second dataset. It is cheap, it is interpretable, and unlike the two proximity features we tried, it actually carries signal.

Space in the model I — heterogeneity

Spatial heterogeneity

The model itself changes across space

So far geography entered as a column. Spatial heterogeneity is different: parts of the model vary with location.

Three things can vary:

varies name meaning
\(\alpha\) spatial fixed effects different baseline level per place
\(\beta\) spatial regimes the relationships differ per place
\(\sigma^2\) spatial heteroskedasticity error variance differs per place

We handle the first two.

Spatial fixed effects

Let the intercept vary by neighbourhood

\[\log P_i = \alpha_r + \sum_k \mathbf{X}_{ik}\beta_k + \epsilon_i\]

We are missing factors that determine price, and some vary systematically by area. Rather than measure them, control for them with one binary per neighbourhood.

This is sometimes called space as a proxy: we do not know what makes a neighbourhood desirable, but we know the pattern it follows, and we can let \(\alpha\) absorb it.

And note what it is

A spatial fixed effect is exactly the clustering-as-a-feature idea from the previous section, with administrative boundaries supplying the labels.

Spatial regimes

Let the slopes vary too

\[\log P_i = \alpha_r + \sum_k \mathbf{X}_{ki}\beta_{k-r} + \epsilon_i\]

Fixed effects assume the relationships are the same everywhere and only the baseline shifts. Regimes relax that: every coefficient may differ by group.

The substantive claim is stronger. Not just “the coast is more expensive”, but “an extra bedroom is worth a different amount on the coast than inland.”

Regimes, read

What the two columns say

Compare the two columns rather than reading either alone. Where they are close, the regime split bought nothing for that variable. Where they diverge, the relationship genuinely differs between coast and inland.

The cost of regimes is parameters. You have doubled the coefficient count, so each is estimated on half the data and standard errors grow. Split on a variable with no real effect and you have added noise and lost power for nothing.

Regimes work when the split is theoretically motivated — here, that the ocean changes how people value a property — not when it is chosen because it improved the fit.

Space in the model II — dependence

Spatial dependence

The configuration itself matters

Heterogeneity says the model differs by place. Dependence says something stronger: the outcome at one location depends on what is at other locations.

A listing’s price may depend not only on whether it is a townhouse, but on whether it is surrounded by townhouses or by high-rises. Hosts price against their neighbours; the neighbours price against them.

Three standard ways to encode this, differing in where the neighbour term enters.

Three models

Where does \(W\) go?

model equation the claim
SLX \(y = \alpha + X\beta + WX\gamma + \epsilon\) my neighbours’ features affect me
Spatial error \(y = \alpha + X\beta + u\), \(u = \lambda Wu + \epsilon\) the unmeasured stuff is spatially correlated
Spatial lag \(y = \alpha + \rho Wy + X\beta + \epsilon\) my neighbours’ outcome affects me

These are different substantive theories, not three fits of one idea. Choosing between them is a modelling decision, and the diagnostics only partly settle it.

SLX — exogenous effects

The simplest: add spatial lags of the covariates

Put the dependence in the unexplained part

\[y = \alpha + X\beta + u, \qquad u = \lambda W u + \epsilon\]

The claim: we have the right covariates, but whatever we failed to measure is spatially correlated. Fix the errors and the coefficients become trustworthy again.

A non-zero \(\lambda\) confirms residual dependence. Note this model does not change what the covariates mean — it corrects their standard errors.

Spatial lag

Put the dependence in the outcome

\[y = \alpha + \rho W y + X\beta + \epsilon\]

m8 = spreg.GM_Lag(
    db[["log_price"]].values,
    db[variable_names].values,
    w=knn,
    name_y="log_price",
    name_x=variable_names,

print(f"rho = {m8.betas.flatten()[-1]:.4f}")

rho = 0.3416

\(\rho\) is the strength of the feedback: how much a neighbour’s price moves mine.

And \(y\) is on both sides

That is not a typo — it is what makes this model special, and what makes its coefficients mean something different.

Why lag coefficients are not slopes

Feedback loops

In OLS, \(\beta_k\) is the effect of changing \(X_k\) by one unit. In a spatial lag model it is not, because changing \(X\) at one location changes \(y\) there, which changes \(Wy\) at the neighbours, which changes their \(y\), which feeds back.

Effects therefore decompose:

  • direct — the effect on \(i\) of changing \(X_i\), including the feedback that returns to \(i\)
  • indirect (spillover) — the effect on everyone else
  • total — their sum

Reading \(\beta\) from a lag model as though it were a slope is simply wrong, and it is a common error. Report the direct and indirect effects instead.

Validating spatial predictions

The question nobody asked

Everything so far was in-sample

Every \(R^2\) in this lecture describes how well a model fits the data it was fitted on. For explanation that is defensible. For prediction it is not, and prediction is what you will usually be asked for.

The standard answer is cross-validation: hold out a fold, fit on the rest, score on the held-out part, repeat.

We are about to see that the standard answer does not work on spatial data, for a reason this lecture has already established.

Recall the diagnostic

We measured this in part 2

Residuals are spatially autocorrelated: \(\text{corr}(u, Wu) \approx 0.33\) at \(k=20\). Nearby listings have similar prices and similar errors.

Now consider what random \(k\)-fold cross-validation does with that. It shuffles rows and cuts them into five parts. Every test listing has, almost certainly, several of its immediate neighbours in the training set.

So the model is not predicting an unseen location. It is interpolating between points it has already seen — and interpolation is easy.

This is leakage

The same failure as Bio 2, in different clothing

not independent random CV therefore
taxonomy strains within a genus tests on near-duplicates of training sequences
single cell cells within a donor treats one donor as thousands of replicates
geography nearby places tests on the interior of the training set

The rule is the same in all three: split at the level you want to generalise to. If you want to predict prices in a district you have no data for, your folds must be districts.

Setting up an honest test

Features, target, and a projected CRS

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, KFold, GroupKFold

dbm = db.to_crs(epsg=3857)          # metres, so blocks are metres
xy = numpy.column_stack((dbm.geometry.x, dbm.geometry.y))
y = dbm["log_price"].values

house = ["accommodates", "bathrooms", "bedrooms", "beds"]


def rf():
    return RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=120, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1)


def blocks(size):
    """Assign each listing to a square block of `size` metres."""
    bx = ((xy[:, 0] - xy[:, 0].min()) // size).astype(int)
    by = ((xy[:, 1] - xy[:, 1].min()) // size).astype(int)
    return bx * 10_000 + by

Reproject first. Blocks must be defined in metres; dividing degrees by 4000 is meaningless.

Isolating the leak

Three models, two validation schemes

g = blocks(4000)
rows = []

]:
    r = cross_val_score(rf(), X, y, cv=KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0),
                        scoring="r2").mean()
    b = cross_val_score(rf(), X, y, cv=GroupKFold(5), groups=g, scoring="r2").mean()

Read that table again

The coordinates-only model is the proof

Under random CV a model using nothing but latitude and longitude scores \(R^2 \approx 0.17\). It looks like it learned something about San Diego.

Under block CV the same model scores negative — worse than predicting the mean price for every listing.

All of its apparent skill was memorising neighbours. Remove the neighbours from the training set and there is nothing left, because there was never anything there.

And the contrast

house features only barely moves between the two schemes. Bedrooms and bathrooms are not spatial, so blocking costs nothing. The gap belongs to the coordinates, and only to them.

How big are the blocks?

Reading the curve

What this curve actually shows

Read the numbers, not the story you expected.

At 250 m the block score equals random CV (0.656 vs 0.653). Blocks that small barely separate anything — a 250 m block still leaves neighbours in the training set. That agreement is a useful sanity check: it confirms the mechanism is distance, not the GroupKFold machinery.

The trend is downward but not monotone. 1 000 m scores below 2 000 m here. With five folds over a few hundred blocks, each estimate carries real variance, and a single sweep is noisy. Repeat with several random block origins before reading much into any one point.

It has not flattened by 8 km. San Diego spans roughly 40 km, so even 8 km blocks leave training listings fairly close to test ones. The honest conclusion is that we have not yet reached the distance at which leakage stops.

So what do you report?

Not a plateau, because we do not have one. Report the score at the block size matching your intended use: if you will predict 5 km from your nearest training data, the 4–8 km numbers are the relevant ones — around 0.57–0.60, against the 0.65 that random CV advertises.

The optimism here is modest precisely because most of this model’s signal is non-spatial. That is the reassuring case. The coordinates-only model on the previous slide is the other one.

Buffered leave-one-out

A stricter variant

Blocks still put some test points right next to a block boundary, and so next to training data. Buffered LOO removes that: hold out one observation, and also delete from the training set everything within radius \(r\) of it.

for i in range(n):
    d = distances_from(i)
    train = (d > buffer_radius)
    model.fit(X[train], y[train])
    pred[i] = model.predict(X[[i]])

Expensive — one fit per observation — but it gives a clean answer to “how well do I predict at distance \(r\) from any data?”, which is often the actual question.

Checking the residuals again

Close the loop

The diagnostic that opened this lecture is also the one that closes it. After fitting any predictive model, compute Moran’s I on its residuals.

  • Significant autocorrelation remaining → there is spatial structure the model has not captured. Consider the features and models from parts 3 and 4.
  • No autocorrelation → the spatial structure is accounted for, and the i.i.d. assumption behind your inference is defensible.

It costs three lines and it is the single most informative check you can run on a spatial model.

The thread

One lesson, three courses

Geo 5 and 6 taught you to measure spatial dependence. This lecture used it twice: once to improve models, and once to distrust them.

The second use is the transferable one. Spatial autocorrelation is a specific case of the general problem that observations are not independent — the same problem as related organisms in Bio 2 and cells from one donor in Bio 4.

Before trusting a score, ask what the unit of independence actually is.
