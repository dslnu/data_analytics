The spine

Seven steps

  1. Provenance and structure — where did this come from, and what shape is it in?
  2. Measurement — what do the values measure, and what does absence mean?
  3. Estimand — what number am I trying to learn, and what makes it identifiable?
  4. Estimation — which estimator, and why that one?
  5. Uncertainty — how wrong could this be, and from how many sources?
  6. Evaluation — how would I know if it were wrong?
  7. Delivery — can someone else get this number again, and who acts on it?

Step 0 — Framing

The rubric starts at Step 1 with a dataset already in hand. That is a real gap. Nothing in the seven steps asks whether this is the right question.

CoNVO, from Shron’s Thinking with Data — written before a dataset is chosen:

  • Context — whose problem is this, and what are they trying to achieve?
  • Need — what is broken that this fixes? A deficiency in the world, not a request for a technique.
  • Vision — a sketch of the finished artefact.
  • Outcome — how it gets used, and how you would know a year later that it mattered.

Then the mockup: draw the final figure, with invented numbers, before touching data. If you cannot draw it, you do not yet know what the project is.

This is the same discipline as estimand-before-estimator, one level up. Left to themselves students propose a dataset and a method, which is backwards, and the error stays invisible until the project fails in week 15.

The mockup is the item to enforce hardest in the week 9 proposal. Fifteen minutes with a pen versus a failed capstone.

Say plainly that CoNVO is not a substitute for an identification argument — Shron’s causality chapter is thin and weeks 2 to 4 supersede it.

An analysis is an argument

Shron imports Toulmin’s structure. It maps onto the spine almost cell for cell.

Toulmin Spine In practice
Claim 3–4 The estimate
Evidence 1–2 The data, and what it measures
Warrant 3 The identification assumptions
Backing 3 Why you believe the warrant: DAG, mechanism, domain knowledge
Qualifier 5 The uncertainty, from all its sources
Rebuttal 5–6 Falsification, negative controls, sensitivity analysis

Conditional exchangeability is a warrant: the unstated bridge that licenses moving from evidence to claim, and the first thing an opponent attacks.

An untestable assumption is not a defect of the method. It is the load-bearing element of the argument.

This slide is for the students who find the formalism alienating. It gives them a rhetorical vocabulary for the thing the spine does statistically, and the warrant row is the one that changes how they read papers.

It also sharpens peer review in week 16: “which warrant is weakest, and what backing would strengthen it” beats “critique this analysis” by a wide margin.

Step 1 — Provenance and structure

The question

Where did these numbers come from, who produced them, why, and what did they gain or lose by producing them? And what shape is the result in?

Two concerns, one work session: you read the codebook, load the data, model the schema, and write the validation rules in a single sitting.

Data are not found objects

Someone chose an instrument, a sampling frame, a coding scheme, a retention policy, and a release schedule — usually for reasons unrelated to your analysis.

  • Administrative data exist to run a process, not to be analysed. Categories change when the process changes.
  • Survey data carry a design: strata, clusters, weights. Ignoring it gives wrong point estimates and badly wrong standard errors.
  • Sensor data carry an instrument: calibration drift, saturation, missing scans.
  • Derived data carry someone else’s model, with its own assumptions baked in.

The reanalysis products in week 5 are the clearest case of the last one: ERA5 is not observation, it is a physical model constrained by observation. Students who treat it as ground truth will over-trust it in exactly the regions where observations are sparse.

Five questions, in writing, every project

  1. What is the unit of observation, precisely?
  2. What is the target population, and how does the sampling frame differ from it?
  3. What was measured, and what was derived, imputed, or modelled upstream?
  4. Has this been revised since publication, and does the archive keep the vintage you need?
  5. If someone had an incentive to influence these numbers, how would they do it?

These are graded. A project that answers them badly cannot recover in later steps.

Question 2, elaborated: frame versus population

The sampling frame is what you can draw from. The target population is what you want to speak about. They are never the same.

Domain Frame Population Gap
Astronomy Bright, nearby objects All objects Malmquist bias
Genomics European-ancestry cohorts Humans Portability failure of PGS
Healthcare Patients who presented People with the condition Berkson-type selection
NLP Documents a platform retained Discourse Moderation and deletion

In week 6 you will estimate the astronomy version of this gap directly, by injecting synthetic signals and counting recoveries.

Question 4, elaborated: vintages

Macroeconomic series are revised. Fundamental data are restated. Satellite archives are reprocessed. Clinical codes are back-filled.

Using the final vintage when your analysis would have seen the initial release is a silent and severe error. FRED’s ALFRED archive exists precisely to make this checkable.

The general rule: point-in-time data. What was knowable, when.

This is the look-ahead bias problem in week 9 and it recurs in energy forecasting in week 13 as the weather-forecast-versus-weather-actual distinction. Flag the connection now.

Structure: schema is a modelling decision

You already know SQL, so this moves fast. Given three incompatible sources, what is the right target schema — and what does “right” mean here?

  • Dimensional modelling; entity-relationship reasoning over messy sources
  • What is an entity and what is an attribute (a judgement with real consequences)
  • Tidy long format versus wide analysis matrix, and when to move between them
  • Schema evolution: what happens when a source adds a column

And the one most people have never met:

Valid time versus transaction time

Valid time — when the fact was true in the world.

Transaction time — when your database learned it.

What did we believe about this supplier’s ownership on the date the contract was awarded?

A non-bitemporal warehouse cannot answer that question. Every audit, compliance, and backtesting application needs it.

This is week 11 material but plant it now. Snodgrass is free online and is the best treatment. The connection to point-in-time financial data on the previous slide is exact: they are the same idea discovered independently by two communities.

Contracts: validation that fails, not warns

import pandera as pa

schema = pa.DataFrameSchema({
    "patient_id":  pa.Column(str, unique=True),
    "admit_date":  pa.Column("datetime64[ns]"),
    "discharge":   pa.Column("datetime64[ns]", nullable=True),
    "age":         pa.Column(int, pa.Check.in_range(0, 120)),
}, checks=pa.Check(lambda df: (df.discharge >= df.admit_date).all()))

A schema test checks types and ranges. A semantic test checks that the data mean something coherent — discharge after admission, weights summing to a population, a hierarchy that actually nests.

Both belong in the pipeline, and both should stop it.

Step 2 — Measurement and missingness

The question

What do the recorded values actually measure, and what does absence mean?

This is where most of you are weakest, because undergraduate data arrives pre-cleaned.

Absence is a measurement

A laboratory value is absent because nobody ordered the test.

Nobody ordered the test because the clinician was not worried.

The clinician’s worry is what you are trying to predict.

The missingness is more informative than the value.

Add an indicator for whether each test was ordered and predictive performance jumps. Then ask what happens when the hospital changes its ordering protocol.

That last question is the whole lesson. The indicator is real signal and it is also a dependency on an institutional practice that can change overnight. This is week 8 and it recurs in week 12 as implicit feedback.

MCAR, MAR, MNAR — and the limits

MCAR — missingness independent of everything. Almost never true; testable in principle.

MAR — missingness depends only on observed data. This is what multiple imputation requires, and it is not testable from the data you have.

MNAR — missingness depends on the unobserved value itself. Imputation is invalid; you need a model of the missingness mechanism or you need bounds.

The taxonomy’s real limitation: MAR versus MNAR is an assumption about a mechanism, and no diagnostic distinguishes them. Argue for it, do not test for it.

The same problem in three domains

Domain Absence means Naive treatment Consequence
Healthcare Test not ordered Impute the mean Erases the clinician’s judgement
Recommenders Item not clicked Treat as negative Confounds exposure with preference
Knowledge graphs Edge not asserted Treat as false Closed-world assumption, silently

Three literatures, one problem: the absence of a record is being read as evidence of absence.

This slide ties weeks 8, 11 and 12 together and it is worth dwelling on. The knowledge-graph row is the crispest formal statement, because the KR tradition named the assumption explicitly and the other two communities did not.

Measurement error: two kinds

Classical. \(X_{obs} = X_{true} + \varepsilon\), with \(\varepsilon\) independent of \(X_{true}\). In simple regression this attenuates the coefficient toward zero. Students find this reassuring and then over-generalise it.

Berkson. \(X_{true} = X_{obs} + \varepsilon\) — the assigned or group-level value is the observed one, and individual deviation is the error. Occurs with dose assignment, area-level exposure, and any group-mean proxy. It does not attenuate.

With multiple mismeasured covariates, the direction of bias is not predictable at all. “Attenuation means my estimate is conservative” is false in general.

Construct validity

Does the proxy measure the concept?

  • Nightlights as a proxy for economic activity
  • Clicks as a proxy for preference
  • Test scores as a proxy for learning
  • Publication counts as a proxy for research quality
  • Expected goals as a proxy for chance quality

Two ways to validate a construct you cannot observe: convergent (does it agree with an independent measure of the same thing?) and criterion (does it predict something it should predict?).

In week 10 you will build a construct from scratch and measure how well humans agree with each other about it before asking whether a model agrees with humans.

Censoring and truncation are different

Censoring — the unit is in your sample, but the value is only bounded. A drive that has not failed by the end of observation has survived at least that long.

Truncation — the unit is not in your sample at all, because of its value. Objects fainter than the detection limit simply do not appear.

Dropping censored units biases you. Treating them as negative outcomes biases you differently and worse. Week 15 is built on this.

Step 3 — Estimand and identification

The question

What number am I trying to learn, defined without reference to any estimation procedure?

Twenty percent of the capstone. The largest single weight in the rubric.

A model can predict beautifully on the world as it is and be arbitrarily wrong about the world as it would be under intervention. \(P(Y \mid X)\) and \(P(Y \mid do(X))\) coincide only under conditions you must argue for and can never observe.

The sentence you must write first

I want to estimate the average change in \(Y\) that would result from setting \(X\) from \(x_0\) to \(x_1\), in population \(P\), at time \(t\).

You are forbidden from writing code until this sentence exists.

Only then does the question of method arise. This discipline comes from the clinical trials literature — ICH E9(R1) — and it is the single most valuable habit this course can give you.

Enforce this literally in the first problem set. Require the sentence at the top of the submission. It feels bureaucratic for two weeks and then becomes automatic.

Potential outcomes

For unit \(i\) and binary treatment \(A_i \in \{0,1\}\), define \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\): what would be observed under each treatment.

\[Y_i = A_i Y_i(1) + (1 - A_i) Y_i(0)\]

Exactly one is ever observed.

Holland’s fundamental problem: causal inference is a missing data problem where half the data are missing by construction.

Mathematics students take to this quickly. The difficulty is never technical — it is that the central object is not defined in terms of data, and they are unused to a quantity no dataset contains.

Which average?

Estimand Definition When you want it
ATE \(E[Y(1) - Y(0)]\) Universal policy
ATT \(E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 1]\) Should we have done what we did?
CATE \(E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]\) Targeting, personalisation
LATE Effect among compliers What an instrument gives you

These differ, often substantially. Confusing them is common and consequential.

In week 7 you will estimate a LATE via Mendelian randomisation and have to argue about whether the complier subpopulation is the one anyone cares about.

Three assumptions

1. Consistency / SUTVA. The observed outcome is the potential outcome under the received treatment, and one unit’s treatment does not affect another’s. Interference violates the second half — pervasive in networks, epidemics, markets, and electricity grids.

2. Conditional exchangeability. \(\{Y(1), Y(0)\} \perp A \mid X\). This does the work. It is never testable. Everything in the design of an observational study is an argument for it.

3. Positivity. \(0 < P(A = 1 \mid X = x) < 1\). Unlike the second, this is checkable — plot the propensity distribution by arm, every time.

Graphs: the three structures

CONFOUNDER          COLLIDER            MEDIATOR

    L                   A                   A
   / \                   \                   \
  v   v                   v                   v
  A-->Y               C <--                   M
                     ^                         \
                    Y                           v
                                                Y

Adjust for the confounder. Do not adjust for the collider — conditioning on a common effect creates association where none existed. Do not adjust for the mediator if you want the total effect.

The collider case must be taught with a simulation, not an argument. Four lines: generate two independent variables, select on their sum exceeding a threshold, compute the correlation in the selected subsample. It comes out strongly negative and the magnitude surprises people.

“Among survivors”, “among hospitalised patients”, “among funded startups”, “among published studies” are all this.

The back-door criterion

A set \(Z\) suffices to identify the effect of \(A\) on \(Y\) if it blocks every back-door path and contains no descendant of \(A\).

This turns “which variables should I control for?” from a matter of taste into a question with a defensible answer given stated assumptions.

Mechanise it: dagitty or DoWhy will enumerate valid adjustment sets from a graph you draw. The work is in drawing the graph honestly.

The folk rule “adjust for everything you have” is wrong. So is “adjust for everything pre-treatment” — see M-bias.

Step 4 — Estimation

The question

Given the estimand and the identification argument, which estimator — and why that one?

Ten percent of the rubric. The shortest block in this lecture.

You arrive over-weighting this step. The design deliberately deflates it.

Regression as adjustment is not regression as prediction

Same arithmetic. Different object.

As prediction: you want \(\hat{E}[Y \mid X]\) to be accurate out of sample. Regularise freely. Include whatever helps.

As adjustment: you want one coefficient to carry a causal interpretation. Functional form misspecification biases it. Variable selection must follow the graph, not the fit.

And the coefficient on a control variable is not itself interpretable as an effect. This is the Table 2 fallacy and it is everywhere in the applied literature.

Regularisation breaks inference

A LASSO coefficient is a biased, non-linear, data-dependent quantity with no standard sampling distribution.

It is not an estimate of anything in particular, and the naive confidence interval around it does not cover.

If you need both selection and inference, use tools built for it: double machine learning with cross-fitting, or post-selection inference. Do not report LASSO coefficients with p-values.

Chernozhukov et al. (2018) is the reference. The Neyman-orthogonality plus cross-fitting construction is genuinely elegant and this audience will appreciate it in week 8.

Doubly robust estimation

Consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correct.

\[\hat{\tau}_{AIPW} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left[ \hat{\mu}_1(X_i) - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i) + \frac{A_i(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_1(X_i))}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1-A_i)(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i))}{1 - \hat{e}(X_i)} \right]\]

Two chances to be right instead of one. The machinery — influence functions, cross-fitting — connects directly to theory you have already seen.

It is the modern default, and EconML implements it approachably.

But with poor overlap, a flexible outcome model quietly extrapolates into regions with no comparable units. The estimator should follow from the identification argument, not from a leaderboard — that sentence is the whole grading criterion for this step.

Step 5 — Uncertainty

The question

How wrong could this be, and from how many sources?

You arrive with Bayesian machinery, which is an advantage. The gap is elsewhere.

Sampling variability is the smallest source

In most applied work, the standard error is the least of your problems.

  • Specification uncertainty — which of forty defensible models did you fit?
  • Search uncertainty — how many did you try before this one?
  • Measurement uncertainty — how good is the proxy?
  • Cleaning uncertainty — which rows did you drop, and why?

None of these appear in a confidence interval. All of them typically dominate it.

Four intervals, routinely confused

Interval Covers Shrinks with \(n\)?
Confidence A parameter, over repeated sampling Yes
Credible A parameter, given prior and data Yes
Prediction A future observation No
Tolerance A stated proportion of the population No

The prediction interval does not shrink to zero because the irreducible noise does not go away. Forecasting audiences want this one and analysts hand them the first one.

Multiverse and specification curves

Enumerate every defensible analytical choice — inclusion criteria, transformations, covariate sets, outlier rules — and run all the combinations.

Plot the resulting estimates sorted by magnitude, with the choices shown underneath.

If the conclusion survives the multiverse, you have something. If it appears in eleven of four hundred specifications, you have a specification.

Week 8’s excess mortality project does this concretely: five to ten defensible baseline models produce materially different totals, and published figures almost never report the range. Comparing your range to a published point estimate is a bracing exercise.

Multiplicity, in disguise

Explicit. A GWAS tests a million hypotheses. Everyone corrects. Week 7.

Implicit. You tried a few feature sets, adjusted a window, dropped a bad year, and reported what worked. Same procedure — but the search was informal, so nobody counted it.

Collective. A field tries ten thousand strategies and publishes the survivors. Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue the effective threshold for a new asset-pricing factor should be a \(t\)-statistic near 3, not 2, because of the size of the collective search.

Sensitivity: quantifying “there might be confounding”

Every causal claim in this course must answer: how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to explain this away?

  • E-value — the minimum association strength, on the risk ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need with both exposure and outcome. One number, easy to report, comparable against measured covariates.
  • Rosenbaum bounds — for matched designs.
  • Cinelli–Hazlett — omitted variable bias in regression, with contour plots.

This converts a vague worry into a quantity a reader can evaluate. Report it.

Calibration and proper scoring

A probability is calibrated if, among cases assigned 0.1, about ten percent occur.

Discrimination and calibration are independent. A model can rank perfectly and be systematically overconfident, or be well calibrated and useless for ranking.

Use a proper scoring rule — Brier, log score, CRPS — which is minimised by the true distribution and therefore cannot be gamed by shading your forecasts.

Week 13 pushes this furthest: sharpness subject to calibration is the objective, and you will find you have been getting it backwards.

Step 6 — Evaluation

The question

How do you know the answer is good, using a test the answer could actually fail?

The emphasis is on could fail. Most reported evaluations are constructed so that they cannot.

The random split is usually the wrong split

A random train-test split assumes exchangeable rows. Almost no real dataset has them.

Dependence Correct split Chapter
Temporal Train on past, test on future Healthcare, finance, energy
Spatial Blocked by region Geoscience, mobility
Group / cluster Hold out whole patients, outlets, drives Healthcare, NLP, reliability
Hierarchical Hold out whole donors or sites Genomics

The amount by which the wrong split flatters you is a measurable quantity. You will measure it at least three times this semester.

Three tiers: internal (resampling within development data — necessary, weakest), temporal (a later period, catching drift), external (a different site, cohort or instrument — almost always worse than expected, and that gap is the interesting number).

Leakage: a taxonomy

1. Outcome-adjacent features. A variable recorded at or after the moment the outcome is determined.

2. Treatment as predictor. The operator already recognised the risk and acted. The model learns to predict the operator, and fails when protocol changes.

3. Preprocessing leak. Imputation, scaling, or feature selection fitted before the split. Small, silent, present in a large fraction of student projects.

4. Duplicate leak. The same entity in both splits under different identifiers — which is why Step 1’s entity resolution is an evaluation problem too.

Negative controls and falsification

The strongest evaluations are the ones designed to fail.

  • Negative control outcome — something your exposure could not plausibly affect. If you find an effect, your design is broken.
  • Negative control exposure — something that could not plausibly cause your outcome.
  • Placebo timing — run the analysis on a date where nothing happened.
  • Scrambling — destroy the structure you claim to detect and rerun. Any signal that survives is an artefact of the algorithm.

Cheap, powerful, and almost never done unless required. They are required here.

Measure your own instrument

Injection-recovery. Inject synthetic signals of known strength into real data, run your unmodified pipeline, and count recoveries as a function of signal strength.

The result is a completeness map: what your method would have found. Without it, “small effects are rare” and “small effects are hard to detect” are indistinguishable.

Astronomy does this routinely. Almost no other field does. Everyone should.

Week 6 is built entirely around this. Split the class: half compute an occurrence rate without the completeness correction, half with. Compare in the same session. It is the most efficient demonstration of selection bias available in any domain.

Evaluation must match the decision

AUC is a ranking statistic averaged over all thresholds, including ones nobody would use.

Decision curve analysis asks: at the threshold you would actually deploy, does using this model beat treating everyone and treating no one?

A model with AUC 0.82 can show zero net benefit at every plausible threshold. Students find this genuinely surprising and it permanently changes what they report.

Step 7 — Reproducibility and delivery

The question

Can someone else — including you in six months — obtain this number again? And who is going to act on it?

Everything that leaves your hands: the artefact and the claim.

The one-command rule

$ make all

regenerates every number, table, and figure in the report from raw inputs.

Not “the notebook runs.” Not “here are the cells in order.”

One command, from a clean checkout, on a machine that is not yours.

Enforce from problem set one. Far cheaper to establish in week 5 than to retrofit in week 15, and the capstone rubric allocates 10 percent to it.

The parts you have not seen

You know Git and Docker, so this is brisk.

  • Data versioning — DVC, or content-addressed snapshots with checksums in the repository. A subset must be reproducible from the recipe, never shipped as a file of unknown provenance.
  • Explicit dependency graphs — Snakemake or a plain Makefile. The point is the graph, not the scheduler.
  • Environment pinning that actually pins — a lockfile, not a range.
  • Seeds — and the fact that stochastic algorithms are not reproducible across library versions even with a fixed seed.

Delivery: a claim, not a metric

“The model achieved an AUC of 0.83.”

“Among patients admitted through the emergency department, this score identifies a high-risk decile with roughly four times the baseline readmission rate. At the threshold the ward would use, it flags about 60 patients per month, of whom around 18 are readmitted. It has not been validated outside this hospital.”

The second is the same model. It is also actionable, falsifiable, and honest about scope.

Nulls are results — report the interval, not the failure to reject. Ranges beat points — when the specification curve is wide, the range is the finding. And write for a reader who stops after the first paragraph, because they will.

The ethics of a ranked list

In week 11 you will produce a ranked list of entities suspected of procurement collusion.

At a base rate of one to five percent, and with your measured true and false positive rates, most of your top fifty is innocent.

You will compute and report that number before naming a single entity. The responsible output is a prioritised review queue with the base rate attached — not an accusation.

This generalises to every screening application you will ever build.

Infrastructure

Laptop-first, deliberately

Every project runs on 16 GB of RAM, four cores, no GPU, within a working session.

This is a pedagogical choice, not a limitation.

A course where every project begins with “spin up a cluster” teaches you to reach for infrastructure before thinking. The other course inoculates you against that. This one must not undo it.

Making large data fit: three techniques

1. Columnar formats with pushdown. Convert to Parquet; read only the columns and row groups you need. A 200 GB CSV frequently becomes a 4 GB Parquet file you never fully read.

2. DuckDB over files. Query Parquet, CSV, and remote HTTP ranges directly in SQL, with out-of-core execution. You already know SQL, so this is free capability.

3. Principled subsetting. Bounding boxes, time windows, seeded stratified samples — with the recipe committed, not the file.

When you may use the cluster

There is a modest HPC allocation. Justify each request. Four legitimate reasons:

  1. Irreducible scale — subsetting would change the scientific question.
  2. Embarrassingly parallel simulation — the best use of a small cluster, and the recommended way to validate a method.
  3. The one transformer fine-tune in week 10, which has a CPU fallback.
  4. Once, for calibration — so you have felt the difference between eleven hours and eleven minutes.

The contract

Accuracy is not graded

Model performance appears nowhere in the capstone rubric.

A capstone that finds a null result, correctly, with a good design, earns full marks.

Say this twice. Nobody believes it the first time, because three years of coursework taught the opposite. Put it in writing in the handbook and refer back when the first problem set is returned.

Causal inference is the gate

Weeks 2–4 are an intensive block: potential outcomes, DAGs, confounding and colliders, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, sensitivity analysis.

Examined in week 4, separately and early. Twenty percent.

Format: four scenarios described in words. Draw the DAG, name the estimand, name the identification strategy, name the threat, specify one falsification test. No estimation required.

Students who can do this can learn any estimator later. Students who cannot will misuse every estimator they learn.

Defect hunts

Every laboratory notebook contains at least one seeded defect.

A leaked feature. A join that silently duplicates rows. A random split where a grouped split is required. An imputation that destroys the signal.

You find it, name it against the spine, and fix it. Ten percent of the grade.

Assume every notebook I give you is broken, because it is.
