The search problem

Defining the problem

What we are actually asking

Given a query sequence of unknown function, find sequences in an annotated reference database that are homologous to it — and transfer their annotations as a hypothesis about the query.

This is how most sequences ever get a function assigned. Nobody characterised them experimentally; they resemble something that was characterised.

The whole enterprise rests on one inference: sequence similarity implies shared ancestry implies shared function. Each of those implications can fail.

Similarity, as the book defines it

\[\text{similarity} = 1 - \text{Hamming distance of the aligned sequences}\]

the fraction of aligned positions that agree. Similarity is continuous; homology is binary. “80% homologous” is a category error — what is meant is “80% similar, and therefore hypothesised to be homologous”.

The book’s setting: 16S and taxonomy

What the reference database looks like

The book searches 16S rRNA sequences — a gene every bacterium carries, used to identify them — against a reference collection (Greengenes, via qiime-default-reference) in which every sequence carries a taxonomy string:

k__Bacteria; p__Firmicutes; c__Bacilli; o__Lactobacillales; f__Lactobacillaceae; g__Lactobacillus; s__

Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species — an empty g__ means the genus is unknown.

The search, end to end

  1. align the query against every reference sequence
  2. keep the \(n\) best-scoring hits (the book uses \(n = 5\))
  3. read their taxonomies and assign the query the most specific level on which the hits agree

Step 3 is a consensus rule — the same idea as a \(k\)-nearest-neighbour vote, with “back off to a coarser rank” as the tie-breaker.

A detail worth noticing extends the book

The book makes its queries by cutting 200-base slices out of the reference sequences, then searches a random subsample of the same collection. Some queries therefore have their own source sequence in the database — an easier test than a genuinely unknown organism. The book is honest that this is a teaching shortcut; the second half of this lecture shows how much such shortcuts flatter a result.

Homolog, ortholog, paralog

Three words that get used interchangeably and should not be

Homologous — descended from a common ancestral sequence. This is a claim about history, and it is binary: two sequences either share an ancestor or they do not. There is no such thing as “40% homologous”.

Two ways homologs arise, and the distinction matters for what you may conclude:

  • orthologs — separated by a speciation event. Human and mouse haemoglobin. Usually retain the same function, because each species kept one copy doing the same job.
  • paralogs — separated by a duplication within a lineage. Human haemoglobin and myoglobin. One copy is free to drift, so function often diverges.

Why this is a search problem, not a vocabulary problem

Your best BLAST hit may be a paralog. You will then transfer an annotation from a gene that shares ancestry with your query but no longer does the same thing — and nothing in the output warns you.

Similar is not homologous

The inference chain, and where each link breaks

\[\text{similarity} \Rightarrow \text{common ancestry} \Rightarrow \text{shared function}\]

Link 1 fails through convergence and through composition. Two unrelated proteins can arrive at similar sequence independently, and two AT-rich sequences look similar because they are AT-rich. Low-complexity regions — poly-glutamine, repeats — generate high-scoring alignments between things that have nothing to do with each other. This is why BLAST masks them by default (dust, seg).

Link 2 fails through paralogy, through domain shuffling (two proteins sharing one module and nothing else), and through genuine functional divergence after duplication.

The chain is still the best tool available. It is just not a proof, and the output format — a confident table of percentages — makes it look like one.

The database has its own problems

What you are searching against

  • Redundancy. GenBank contains the same gene thousands of times from thousands of strains. A “hit list” of 500 sequences may be 500 copies of one finding. This is why RefSeq and UniRef exist: curated, de-duplicated subsets.
  • Annotation is mostly inferred, not measured. The overwhelming majority of sequence annotations were themselves assigned by homology search. Transfer an annotation and you are often copying a copy.
  • Errors propagate and amplify. A misannotation becomes the best hit for the next query, which inherits it, which becomes evidence for the next. The literature calls this annotation error percolation, and it is measurable in the databases.
  • Sampling is biased. Reference databases over-represent organisms that are easy to culture and interesting to fund. An environmental sample is mostly things nobody has sequenced.

Every one of these makes your results look better than they are, never worse.

Four ways to make it faster

The book’s list

  1. perform fewer alignments — fewer queries (the user will object), a smaller database (it may lose the answer), or skip the alignments that are unlikely to score well
  2. a faster implementation — until it cannot be made faster
  3. a faster computer
  4. parallel processors

Options 2–4 all divide the runtime by some factor \(f\): \(\text{new runtime} \approx \text{runtime} / f\). Only option 1 changes how much work there is, and its third variant — a cheap test that predicts which alignments are worth doing — is what the rest of this half is about.

And the problem is not going away

The book shows two log-scale plots: the cost of sequencing falling, and the size of sequence databases growing. Many people think DNA sequencing is getting cheaper faster than computers are. More queries, against larger databases, on hardware that improves more slowly than both. Waiting for faster machines is not a strategy.

The shape of the solution

Do not align what cannot match

Two homologous sequences share exact short substrings almost certainly. Two unrelated sequences share far fewer, and only by chance.

So: use a cheap, approximate test to discard the vast majority of the database, and run the expensive exact alignment only on what survives.

\[\text{cheap filter} \rightarrow \text{small candidate set} \rightarrow \text{exact alignment}\]

This is seed-and-extend, and it is the core idea of BLAST. It is also the same shape as the sampling and approximation strategies in the scheduling lectures: give up a guarantee, buy several orders of magnitude.

\(k\)-mer similarity

Jaccard index over \(k\)-mer sets

\[J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}\]

where \(A\) and \(B\) are the sets of \(k\)-mers in each sequence. No alignment, no matrix — two set operations.

def kmers(s, k=5):
    return {s[i : i + k] for i in range(len(s) - k + 1)}


def jaccard(a, b):
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b) if (a | b) else 0.0

What the heuristic gives up

It is not a proof

Look again at the “diverged 35%” row. Its Jaccard is close to the unrelated ones — a threshold tuned to exclude noise will also exclude a genuine homolog.

That is the trade in one line: heuristics lose distant homologs, and distant homologs are often the interesting ones.

  • for close matches, the heuristic and the exact algorithm agree
  • for remote homology, only exhaustive search finds it, and nobody can afford exhaustive search

Specialised tools (PSI-BLAST, HMMER profile methods) exist precisely to push the detection limit further out.

Judging a heuristic

The book’s two questions, made into an experiment

A heuristic chooses which references to align against; anything it skips can never be the answer. So measure:

  1. how often is the answer still right?
  2. how much faster is it?

And compare against a straw man — a heuristic so naive that anything worth using must beat it. The book’s straw man aligns against a random 10% of the database.

rng_h = np.random.default_rng(11)


def mutate_h(s, rate):
    return "".join(rng_h.choice(list("ACGT")) if rng_h.random() < rate else c for c in s)


# 40 genera x 5 reference sequences; one new query per genus, cut as a slice
ancestors = {f"g{i}": "".join(rng_h.choice(list("ACGT"), 300)) for i in range(40)}
refs = [(g, mutate_h(a, 0.10)) for g, a in ancestors.items() for _ in range(5)]
queries_h = [(g, mutate_h(a, 0.10)[75:225]) for g, a in ancestors.items()]

ref_kmers = [kmers(s, 8) for _, s in refs]                      # pre-computed once
ref_gc = np.array([(s.count("G") + s.count("C")) / len(s) for _, s in refs])
keep = len(refs) // 10                                           # align against 10%


def pick_random(q):
    return rng_h.choice(len(refs), keep, replace=False)


def pick_gc(q):
    gc = (q.count("G") + q.count("C")) / len(q)
    return np.argsort(np.abs(ref_gc - gc))[:keep]


def pick_kmer(q):
    qk_ = kmers(q, 8)
    return np.argsort([-len(qk_ & rk) for rk in ref_kmers])[:keep]


def search(pick):
    correct = 0
    for g, q in queries_h:
        idx = range(len(refs)) if pick is None else pick(q)
        best = max(idx, key=lambda i: aligner.align(q, refs[i][1]).score)
        correct += refs[best][0] == g
    return correct / len(queries_h)

Precompute the database, not the query

def candidates(query, index, min_shared=150):

With no threshold, every sequence is hit: a random 300-base sequence has ~296 5-mers out of only \(4^5 = 1024\) possible, so unrelated sequences share ~90 by chance. The threshold has to sit above the chance level — here between ~95 and the homolog’s ~220 — and then the filter leaves one alignment to do instead of 201.

This is the data structure inside every fast aligner — BLAST, DIAMOND, minimap2, kraken.

Cost of the index

What you pay, and when

build query
exhaustive alignment nothing \(O(N \cdot mn)\)
inverted index \(O(N \cdot m)\) once \(O(m + \lvert\text{hits}\rvert)\)

The index is built once and amortised over every subsequent search — which is exactly why NCBI can serve BLAST to the world, and why you makeblastdb before you blastn.

The memory cost is real: an index over \(10^{12}\) bases does not fit on a laptop, which is why tools like DIAMOND and minimap2 spend most of their engineering on compressing the index (minimizers, spaced seeds, FM-index) rather than on the alignment itself.

How BLAST actually works

Seed, extend, evaluate

  1. Index the database by \(k\)-mer (word size 11 for nucleotides, 3 for proteins) — done once, offline.
  2. Seed — look up the query’s words to find exact hits. For proteins, also include neighbourhood words scoring above a threshold under BLOSUM62, so a conservative substitution still seeds.
  3. Extend — grow each seed in both directions, stopping when the score drops more than \(X\) below its maximum.
  4. Evaluate — keep extensions above a threshold and report them with a statistical significance estimate.

Almost all of the \(O(mn)\) matrix is never touched. That is where the speed comes from.

Word size is the main dial

One parameter controls the whole trade-off

A seed must be an exact match of length \(w\). So the probability that a homologous region seeds at all is roughly \(p^{w}\), where \(p\) is the per-position identity.

At 80% identity: \(0.8^{11} \approx 0.086\) per position — but with hundreds of positions to try, a seed is nearly certain. At 60% identity: \(0.6^{11} \approx 0.004\), and short homologous regions now seed only occasionally.

word size effect
larger fewer seeds, fewer extensions, faster, misses remote homology
smaller more seeds, more extensions, slower, finds weaker matches

blastn defaults to 11 for general search and 28 for megablast, which is tuned for near-identical sequences and is roughly an order of magnitude faster. If a search “finds nothing”, the word size is the first thing to check — megablast on diverged sequences finds nothing by design.

Spaced seeds

Why contiguous seeds are a poor use of \(w\) positions

A contiguous 11-mer requires 11 consecutive matches. But mutations are roughly independent across positions, so consecutive runs are exactly what diverged sequences lack.

A spaced seed requires matches only at selected positions of a longer window — pattern 111*1**1*1**11*111 demands 11 matches spread over 18 positions. Same cost to look up, far more likely to be satisfied by a genuinely homologous region.

Better still, overlapping hits from a spaced seed are less correlated than from a contiguous one, so a region that fails to seed at one offset often seeds at the next. PatternHunter showed this gives higher sensitivity and higher speed than contiguous seeding — a rare case of no trade-off.

The rule worth remembering: search in protein space whenever you can. The genetic code is redundant, so synonymous substitutions accumulate in DNA while the protein is unchanged. Two genes at 45% nucleotide identity — indistinguishable from noise — can be 80% identical as proteins and obviously homologous.

Protein search also has a 20-letter alphabet, so a chance match is far less likely, and BLOSUM62 lets a conservative substitution still count.

Bit scores

The score you can actually compare

A raw score \(S\) depends on the scoring matrix and gap penalties you used, so raw scores are not comparable between searches. The bit score normalises that away:

\[S' = \frac{\lambda S - \ln K}{\ln 2}\]

Bit scores are comparable across searches and databases. The E-value then follows from the bit score and the search space alone:

\[E = \frac{m \, n}{2^{S'}}\]

So: bit score describes the alignment; E-value describes the alignment in the context of this particular database. Report bit score when comparing hits to each other, E-value when arguing that a hit is real.

Minimizers

Indexing every \(k\)-mer is wasteful

A sequence of length \(m\) has \(m - k + 1\) \(k\)-mers, and consecutive ones overlap in \(k-1\) characters — enormously redundant.

A minimizer scheme picks, from each window of \(w\) consecutive \(k\)-mers, only the one that is smallest under some ordering. Two sequences that share a long exact stretch are guaranteed to select the same minimizer within it, so sensitivity is largely preserved.

The index shrinks by roughly a factor of \(w\). This is the trick behind minimap2 and Kraken, and it is why long-read alignment became tractable at all.

The general pattern

Exact \(\rightarrow\) approximate \(\rightarrow\) sampled approximate. Each step trades a guarantee for a constant factor, and each is justified by measuring what the guarantee was worth.

What would a non-homolog score?

The book’s question

An alignment is a hypothesis of homology. So ask: what fraction of the time would I get a score at least this good if the sequences were not homologous? That fraction is a p-value.

We have no supply of guaranteed non-homologs — but we can make them. Shuffle the query: same length, same composition, order destroyed. Any homology is gone; everything that inflates scores by chance (length, base composition) is kept.

It is the same move as shuffling a deck of cards: the cards are unchanged, only their order is lost.

A permutation test

Align against 99 shuffles, and count

rng_p = np.random.default_rng(3)


def shuffled(s):
    return "".join(rng_p.permutation(list(s)))


def permutation_p(query, subject, n=99):
    actual = aligner.align(query, subject).score
    null = np.array([aligner.align(shuffled(query), subject).score for _ in range(n)])
    return actual, null, (np.sum(null >= actual) + 1) / (n + 1)


q_p = "".join(rng_p.choice(list("ACGT"), 150))
for label, subj in [("identical", q_p),
                    ("unrelated", "".join(rng_p.choice(list("ACGT"), 150)))]:
    actual, null, p = permutation_p(q_p, subj)
    print(f"  {label:<10} score {actual:5.0f}   null max {null.max():4.0f}   p = {p:.2f}")
  identical  score   300   null max   22   p = 0.01
  unrelated  score    15   null max   22   p = 0.50

\[p = \frac{\#\{\text{shuffled scores} \ge \text{actual}\} + 1}{n + 1}\]

The \(+1\) counts the actual alignment as one of the possibilities. So with 99 shuffles the smallest attainable p-value is \(1/100\), and a result at that floor is reported as \(p < 0.01\), not \(p = 0.01\) — we have only learned that no shuffle beat it. More shuffles buy more precision.

Choose \(\alpha\) first

Two ways to be wrong, and a dial between them

  • false positive (type 1) — calling non-homologs homologous
  • false negative (type 2) — missing real homologs

\(\alpha\) is the p-value below which we call a result significant. Set it before computing the p-value, so the result cannot influence the threshold. The book’s image: \(\alpha\) is a dial. Turn it up and false positives rise while false negatives fall; turn it down and the reverse.

There is no universally right setting. When would you rather have false positives? When false negatives? That depends on what the result will be used for — a screening step that a second method will check, or a final annotation that will be copied into a database.

Between the extremes

Homologs that have diverged

The interesting cases lie between identical and unrelated. Mutate a copy of the query until only a given fraction of positions is identical:

def with_identity(s, pid):
    return "".join(c if rng_p.random() < pid else rng_p.choice([b for b in "ACGT" if b != c])
                   for c in s)


for pid in (0.95, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25):
    _, _, p = permutation_p(q_p, with_identity(q_p, pid))
    print(f"  {pid:.0%} identical:  p = {p:.2f}   ->  {'homologous' if p < 0.05 else 'not significant'} at alpha = 0.05")
  95% identical:  p = 0.01   ->  homologous at alpha = 0.05
  75% identical:  p = 0.01   ->  homologous at alpha = 0.05
  50% identical:  p = 0.78   ->  not significant at alpha = 0.05
  25% identical:  p = 0.50   ->  not significant at alpha = 0.05

At 25% identity a DNA sequence is no closer to the query than a random one (one base in four agrees by chance), and the test correctly cannot tell.

The 50% row is the instructive one. Those sequences are homologous, yet the test misses them — because the scoring scheme (+2 match, \(-3\) mismatch) is tuned for close matches. At 50% identity the expected score per aligned position is \(0.5 \cdot 2 - 0.5 \cdot 3 < 0\), so no local alignment can grow. The limit of detection belongs to the method as much as to the data.

From permutations to E-values

A raw score means nothing on its own

A score of 60 is impressive against a 100-sequence database and meaningless against \(10^{9}\) — with enough sequences, high scores arise by chance.

Ninety-nine shuffles per hit is affordable for one alignment and absurd for a search returning thousands. BLAST therefore replaces the empirical null with an analytic one (Karlin–Altschul theory), fitted once per scoring scheme.

The E-value is the expected number of alignments scoring at least \(S\) by chance in a database of this size:

\[E = K \, m \, n \, e^{-\lambda S}\]

with \(m\) the query length, \(n\) the database size, and \(K, \lambda\) fitted constants.

  • \(E = 10\) — expect 10 such hits by chance. Noise.
  • \(E = 10^{-5}\) — expect one such hit per 100 000 searches. Interesting.

The E-value grows with database size for a fixed score. The same alignment becomes less significant as the database grows, which is not a flaw — it is the multiple-testing correction being applied honestly.

Percent identity is not enough

The most over-used number in bioinformatics

“These sequences are 85% identical” is nearly meaningless without three more facts:

  • over what length? 85% over 20 residues is noise; over 500 it is strong evidence.
  • relative to which alignment? Percent identity is computed from an alignment, which was produced by a scoring scheme you chose.
  • at what composition? Two AT-rich sequences share bases by chance far more often than two balanced ones.

This is why BLAST reports bit score and E-value alongside identity — and why comparing identity values across papers that used different aligners is not meaningful.

Evaluating a search

Four outcomes

homologous (truth) not homologous
reported true positive false positive
not reported false negative true negative
  • sensitivity (recall) \(= \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\) — what fraction of real homologs did we find?
  • specificity \(= \frac{TN}{TN + FP}\) — what fraction of non-homologs did we correctly ignore?

Lowering the threshold raises sensitivity and lowers specificity. There is no setting that maximises both, so the choice depends on the cost of each error in your application.

mutate redraws a position with probability rate, and a quarter of redraws land on the same base — so a mutation rate of 60% still leaves ~55% of positions identical. Beyond a certain point the signal enters the noise band and no threshold can separate homolog from chance.

The book runs this same sweep with the permutation p-value from a few slides back, and asks the question worth asking of any simulation: what shortcuts were taken to keep it fast, and what would a more robust version change? (more trials per point, more shuffles per p-value, more than one query sequence). This is not a limitation of the heuristic — it is a limitation of the data. The twilight zone for proteins sits around 20–35% identity, and detecting homology below it needs profile methods (PSI-BLAST, HMMER), not better alignment.

Profiles beat pairs

Where the extra information comes from

Pairwise alignment uses two sequences. That is all the evidence it has, and below the twilight zone it is not enough.

A profile uses a whole family. Align many known members, and each column of that alignment tells you something a pair cannot: position 47 is always hydrophobic; position 48 is anything at all. A position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) records that — one score column per position, rather than one matrix for the whole sequence.

The consequence is that a query matching the family’s conserved positions scores well even at 20% overall identity, because the variable positions were never being counted against it in the first place.

Two ways to build a profile

PSI-BLAST and HMMER

PSI-BLAST builds the profile iteratively from your own search. Run blastp, take the hits above a threshold, build a PSSM from them, search again with the PSSM, repeat. Each round reaches sequences the previous round could not see.

The failure mode is specific and worth knowing: if one false positive enters the profile, the next iteration searches for that too. Contamination compounds across rounds — profile drift. Use a strict inclusion threshold and look at what enters on each round.

HMMER uses a profile hidden Markov model, which models position-specific insertion and deletion probabilities as well as substitution — a proper probabilistic model rather than a score table. It is what Pfam is built on, and hmmsearch on a curated Pfam model is generally the most sensitive homology tool available.

And beyond sequence

Below ~20% identity even profiles struggle, and the remaining signal is structural. Structure is conserved far longer than sequence — which is why structure prediction changed what “remote homology detection” means, and why foldseek searching predicted structures now finds relationships no sequence method can.

Machine learning

From alignment to classification

A different framing of the same task

Homology search asks: which database sequence is this most like?

Classification asks: which of these known categories does this sequence belong to? — and answers without comparing against every reference.

The canonical application is taxonomic assignment: given a 16S rRNA sequence from an environmental sample, which genus produced it?

The trick that makes it work is turning a sequence into a fixed-length feature vector, which is exactly what \(k\)-mer counting does.

The feature table

The format every method assumes

Almost every machine-learning method expects one thing:

\[\underbrace{n \text{ samples}}_{\text{rows}} \times \underbrace{p \text{ features}}_{\text{columns}} \;+\; \text{a label per row}\]

That is the only interface. The entire difficulty in applying ML to biology is that biological data does not arrive in this shape, and getting it there is where the decisions are made.

Unsupervised comes first in practice, and for a specific reason: it is how you find out whether your labels correspond to anything in the data at all. If samples do not separate by label in an ordination, a classifier that scores 99% is telling you about leakage, not about biology.

In ecology the unsupervised step is called ordination. It is the same operation you will meet in Bio 3 as dimensionality reduction — different field, different vocabulary, same mathematics.

Distances between samples

Jaccard again, now as a distance

Almost every ordination starts from distances between all pairs of samples. We already have the statistic — the Jaccard index from the search half of this lecture. Subtract it from one:

Ordination

\(n \times n\) distances into two axes

A distance matrix over 120 sequences has 7 140 distinct entries. You cannot read that. Ordination finds a low-dimensional arrangement in which distance on the page approximates distance in the data.

readIAB teaches it twice over — first polar ordination, where the arithmetic is simple enough to do by hand, then PCoA, which is what you would actually use. We will do the same, because the simple version makes clear what the sophisticated one is doing.

Polar ordination by hand

One axis, from two endpoints

Pick the two most distant samples as the endpoints of an axis, then project every other sample onto it by the law of cosines:

\[p_i = \frac{D_{AB}^2 + d(A,i)^2 - d(B,i)^2}{2 D_{AB}}\]

a, b = np.unravel_index(np.argmax(D), D.shape)
Dab = D[a, b]
pos = (Dab**2 + D[a] ** 2 - D[b] ** 2) / (2 * Dab)

print(f"  endpoints    : {ids[a]} and {ids[b]}, distance {Dab:.3f}")
for g in sorted(set(labels)):
    m = np.array(labels) == g
    print(f"  {g}: axis 1 mean {pos[m].mean():7.3f}   sd {pos[m].std():.3f}")

The second axis

The book’s rule: long, and uncorrelated with axis 1

Every pair of samples defines a candidate axis. A good second axis spans a large distance but is uncorrelated with the first — a correlated axis repeats what we already know. “Uncorrelated” is subjective, so the book makes it a score (we add a floor of 0.05 on \(|\rho|\), so that a correlation of almost exactly zero cannot win by dividing by it — the rule then picks the longest of the essentially uncorrelated axes):

\[\text{score}(A) = \frac{\max \text{distance along } A}{\lvert \rho_{\text{Spearman}}(A, \text{axis 1}) \rvert}\]

from scipy.stats import rankdata

i_idx, j_idx = np.triu_indices(len(D), k=1)
ok = D[i_idx, j_idx] > 0
i_idx, j_idx = i_idx[ok], j_idx[ok]
dij = D[i_idx, j_idx][:, None]
axes = (dij**2 + D[i_idx] ** 2 - D[j_idx] ** 2) / (2 * dij)   # one row per candidate axis

r_axes = rankdata(axes, axis=1)
r_axes = (r_axes - r_axes.mean(1, keepdims=True)) / r_axes.std(1, keepdims=True)
r_pos = (rankdata(pos) - rankdata(pos).mean()) / rankdata(pos).std()
rho = (r_axes * r_pos).mean(1)                                 # Spearman, all axes at once
score = dij.ravel() / np.maximum(np.abs(rho), 0.05)            # floor: no division by ~0
second = axes[np.argmax(score)]
print(f"  candidate axes: {len(axes):,}   chosen: |rho| = {abs(rho[np.argmax(score)]):.3f}, "
      f"length {dij.ravel()[np.argmax(score)]:.3f}")
  candidate axes: 7,140   chosen: |rho| = 0.007, length 0.612

Why it is still a teaching device extends the book

The score is a reasonable heuristic, not an optimum, and it was invented for the purpose. PCoA replaces it by choosing all axes at once, orthogonal by construction and ranked by variance explained — which is why the book recommends PCoA in practice.

Reading an ordination plot

Three rules, all of them violated routinely

1. Axis order is meaningful. PC1 explains more than PC2, which explains more than PC3. So the plot is not square: a given distance along PC1 means more than the same distance along PC2. Axes should be scaled by their explained variation, and usually are not.

2. The axes are uncorrelated by construction. PC1 and PC2 are orthogonal, which means they are not two independent biological findings. “Group A separates on PC1 and group B on PC2” describes one decomposition, not two discoveries.

3. The direction of an axis is arbitrary. Eigenvectors are defined up to sign, so flipping an axis changes nothing. Re-run with a different library, or a different seed on a randomised solver, and the plot may be mirrored.

Nothing about “left” or “right” on a PCoA plot means anything. Only relative position does.

Exploration is not confirmation

The book’s most important sentence about ordination

Exploratory analysis is a tool for hypothesis generation, and hypothesis generation and hypothesis testing cannot be performed on the same data.

Colour a PCoA plot by one metadata column after another — species, then phylum, then site, then date — and with enough columns and few enough samples, something will appear to separate. That is a hypothesis, to be tested on new data, not a finding. A pattern found by looking and then “confirmed” by a test on the same samples has been tested against itself.

Two smaller things the book points out

  • Colouring the same ordination by a coarser label (phylum instead of species) often shows tighter grouping — coarser categories are easier to separate. Which label you colour by is a choice that shapes what you see.
  • PCoA on a non-Euclidean distance such as Jaccard can produce negative eigenvalues. Compare their size with the largest positive one; if they are small, ignore them:

Supervised classification

Defining the task, as the book does

Two or more pre-defined classes; assign each observation to one. A spam filter is the everyday example: every email is spam or not spam, and both kinds of mistake have a cost — a full inbox, or a missed message.

Here the classes are microbial taxa, and the classifier replaces the alignment search from the first half: the same question (which organism is this?), answered by a model instead of a database scan.

Training data, test data, cross-validation

  • training data — labelled examples the model learns from; ideally balanced across classes
  • test data — labelled examples it never saw, used only to estimate performance on real-world data
  • never tune on the test set. Train, test, change the model, test again — and the test set has become training data, and its score no longer predicts anything
  • \(k\)-fold cross-validation splits the labelled data \(k\) ways (usually 5 or 10), trains on \(k-1\) parts and tests on the remaining one, \(k\) times — so model development does not consume a dedicated test set

Evaluating a binary classifier

The book’s example: a test for Disease X

has Disease X healthy
test positive true positive (TP) false positive (FP) — type 1
test negative false negative (FN) — type 2 true negative (TN)

\[\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{\text{all}} \qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\]

Why accuracy is not enough

Accuracy cannot say which mistakes a classifier makes. A cheap test with low precision but very high recall almost never misses the disease: a negative result means the patient is healthy, and a positive one means “run the expensive test”. Useless by accuracy, valuable as a screen. Precision and recall, reported as a pair, show which error a classifier tends to make — and which one your application can afford.

Naive Bayes

Why it suits this problem

For a sequence with \(k\)-mer counts \(x\) and class \(c\):

\[P(c \mid x) \propto P(c) \prod_{i} P(x_i \mid c)\]

The “naive” part is that product: it assumes \(k\)-mers occur independently given the class. That is clearly false — overlapping \(k\)-mers share \(k-1\) characters.

It works anyway, for the usual reason: we only need the ranking of \(P(c \mid x)\) across classes to be right, not the probabilities themselves. And it is extremely fast, which matters when classifying millions of reads.

This is the method behind the RDP classifier and q2-feature-classifier, the standard tools in microbiome work.

Naive Bayes, built by hand

The book’s k-mer probability table

\(W\) is every possible \(k\)-mer (\(4^k\) of them). Training counts how many sequences contain each \(k\)-mer — presence, not counts — and adds pseudocounts, so that a \(k\)-mer never seen in training does not zero out a whole product:

\[P_i = \frac{n(w_i) + 0.5}{N + 1} \qquad\qquad P(w_i \mid \text{taxon}) = \frac{m(w_i, \text{taxon}) + P_i}{M_{\text{taxon}} + 1}\]

\(n(w_i)\): training sequences containing \(w_i\), out of \(N\). \(m\), \(M\): the same, within one taxon. The pseudocount in the second formula is scaled by how common the \(k\)-mer is overall.

from itertools import product

W = ["".join(p) for p in product("ACGT", repeat=5)]
w_index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(W)}


def presence_vector(s, k=5):
    v = np.zeros(len(W), dtype=bool)
    v[[w_index[s[i:i + k]] for i in range(len(s) - k + 1)]] = True
    return v


lab = np.array(labels)
train = np.array([i % 40 < 30 for i in range(len(sequences))])  # 30 per genus train, 10 test
V = np.array([presence_vector(s) for s in sequences])

N = train.sum()
P_i = (V[train].sum(0) + 0.5) / (N + 1)
taxa = sorted(set(lab))
log_table = np.array([
    np.log((V[train & (lab == t)].sum(0) + P_i) / ((train & (lab == t)).sum() + 1))
    for t in taxa])                                     # taxa x 4^k
print("k-mer probability table:", log_table.shape)
k-mer probability table: (3, 1024)

Naive Bayes, built by hand

Classifying: sum log-probabilities over the query’s k-mers

\[P(\text{query} \mid \text{taxon}) = \prod_{w \in V} P(w \mid \text{taxon})\]

from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB


def classify_V(v):
    return taxa[int(np.argmax(log_table[:, v].sum(axis=1)))]


ours = np.array([classify_V(v) for v in V[~train]])
sk = MultinomialNB().fit(X[train], lab[train]).predict(X[~train])
print(f"  hand-built NB accuracy : {(ours == lab[~train]).mean():.0%}")
print(f"  agrees with sklearn    : {(ours == sk).mean():.0%} of test sequences")
  hand-built NB accuracy : 100%
  agrees with sklearn    : 100% of test sequences

Working in log space is not optional: the product of hundreds of probabilities below one underflows to zero in floating point. Sums of logs do not.

  mean              : 1.0

Be suspicious of a perfect score

100% accuracy should worry you, not please you. Real taxonomic classifiers do not achieve it, particularly at genus level.

The next slide explains what went wrong here — and it is the single most common mistake in applied machine learning.

The leakage trap

Our test set was never independent

Every sequence labelled Genus_A was generated by mutating one reference sequence at 8%. So any two Genus_A sequences are ~92% identical to the same original, and therefore ~85% identical to each other.

When 5-fold CV splits them randomly, each fold’s test sequences have near-duplicates in the training set. The classifier is not generalising to new organisms; it is recognising sequences it has effectively already seen.

This is data leakage, and it is endemic in biological data because biological data is structured by relatedness: strains within a species, species within a genus, and now sequences within a training set.

What leakage costs you

The failure that matters: an organism you have never seen

Random CV told us 100%. Here is what the same classifier does when handed a genus that was not in its training set at all — the everyday situation in environmental sequencing, where most organisms are uncharacterised.

novel_ref = "".join(np.random.default_rng(999).choice(list("ACGT"), 400))
novel = [mutate(novel_ref, 0.08) for _ in range(10)]

clf_fitted = MultinomialNB().fit(X, labels)
Xn = vec.transform(novel)
pred = clf_fitted.predict(Xn)
conf = clf_fitted.predict_proba(Xn).max(axis=1)

print(f"  mean confidence  : {conf.mean():.3f}   (min {conf.min():.3f}, max {conf.max():.3f})")
  predicted labels : {np.str_('Genus_C'): 10}
  mean confidence  : 0.985   (min 0.854, max 1.000)

Every one of these sequences came from an organism the classifier has never seen. It assigned them all to Genus_C — with a mean reported confidence of 0.985, several of them at 1.000.

The label is meaningless and the confidence is worse than meaningless: it is close to maximal. Depending on the random draw the predictions may land on one genus or scatter across several; what never happens is the classifier declining to answer.

Naive Bayes has no “none of the above”

predict_proba returns a distribution over the classes it was trained on. It is a statement of relative preference among those, not of absolute fit — so it cannot express “this is unlike anything I know”.

Real taxonomic classifiers add a confidence threshold below which they refuse to assign, and report the sequence at a coarser rank (“Bacteria” rather than a genus) or not at all. Without that, a pipeline silently invents plausible answers for every unknown it meets.

The bootstrap confidence

readIAB’s answer, and the real one used by RDP

predict_proba is not the confidence measure taxonomic classifiers actually report. Theirs is a bootstrap: resample the query’s own \(k\)-mers with replacement, reclassify each subsample, and count how often you get the original answer back.

\[\text{confidence} = \frac{\#\{\text{subsamples agreeing with the full query}\}}{\#\text{iterations}}\]

It asks a sharper question than predict_proba does: how much does this assignment depend on which parts of my sequence I happened to look at?

def bootstrap_confidence(clf, counts, n_iter=100, frac=0.1, seed=0):
    """RDP-style confidence: resample the query's k-mers, reclassify, count agreement."""
    rng_b = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    counts = np.asarray(counts).ravel().astype(int)
    occurrences = np.repeat(np.arange(counts.size), counts)
    base = clf.predict(counts.reshape(1, -1))[0]
    size = max(1, int(len(occurrences) * frac))
    agree = sum(
        clf.predict(
            np.bincount(rng_b.choice(occurrences, size, replace=True),
                        minlength=counts.size).reshape(1, -1)
        )[0] == base
        for _ in range(n_iter)
    )
    return base, agree / n_iter

The test that matters

Apply it to a sequence from a known genus, then to the novel ones.

It works — and predict_proba did not

The same predictions, two confidence measures

Both measures describe the identical set of predictions — all ten novel sequences called Genus_C. They disagree completely about how much to believe them.

known genus novel genus
predict_proba 1.000 0.985 (mean of ten)
bootstrap 1.00 0.59 (mean of five)

At the 0.80 threshold conventional in 16S work, all five novel sequences tested (0.51–0.66) are rejected and backed off to a coarser rank. predict_proba would accept every one of them.

Why the bootstrap sees it

Two questions, not one

predict_proba normalises likelihoods across the training classes. Whatever the evidence, the numbers sum to one, and a weak preference and an overwhelming one both come out near 1.0 once the log-likelihoods are exponentiated.

The bootstrap probes something else: how robust is the margin? Take 10% of the query’s \(k\)-mers and the answer either survives or it does not.

  • a genuine Genus_A sequence — even a tenth of its \(k\)-mers point overwhelmingly one way. Confidence 1.00.
  • a novel organism — the preference for Genus_C rests on a handful of coincidental matches. Resample, and a different accident wins. Confidence 0.59.

Fragility under resampling is a usable proxy for “the evidence was thin” — which is what you wanted to know and what the probability would not tell you.

Choosing the threshold from data

What the book does with the confidence scores

A confidence value only becomes useful once you know what good looks like. The book’s recipe uses the labelled test data:

  1. classify every test sequence and record its bootstrap confidence
  2. split the confidences into correct and incorrect assignments and compare the two distributions (a pair of boxplots)
  3. pick the threshold below which a call is reported as unclassified

Then the two questions that make the trade-off concrete: if you never wanted to reject a correct assignment, how often would you accept an incorrect one? If you never wanted to accept an incorrect one, how many correct ones would you throw away?

Finish with a confusion matrix — true taxa on one axis, predicted on the other, in the same order — to see which taxa get confused, and check whether the mistakes are at least right at a coarser level (phylum).

Still not a novelty detector

Do not over-read the result

The bootstrap measures stability, which correlated with fit here. That is not a guarantee:

  • 0.59 is well below threshold but it is not zero — a classifier forced to choose still chose, ten times out of ten, and reported a genus.
  • A novel organism that happens to be consistently similar to one training class will bootstrap high and be confidently wrong. Nothing in the procedure looks outside the label set.

A true novelty measure is a distance to the training data, not a confidence within it. In taxonomic practice that role is played by an alignment-identity floor: assign only if the query is within some identity of a reference.

Which returns us to the first half of this lecture — the classifier does not replace the search, it sits on top of it.

Why this matters here especially

Biological data is never i.i.d.

The independence assumption behind random cross-validation is violated by construction in biology:

  • sequences from the same strain are near-identical
  • strains within a species are highly similar
  • species within a genus share most of their genome
  • and reference databases are biased toward organisms people study

A classifier can score 99% on a benchmark and fail completely on an environmental sample full of organisms nobody has sequenced.

Regard these methods as a starting point rather than an end-point — the same warning that will return in Bio 4 for automated cell-type annotation. It is the same underlying problem.

Splitting by group

The fix: make the split respect the structure

Random \(k\)-fold CV asks can the model classify a new sequence? What you almost always want to know is can it classify a new organism? — and that is a different question, answered by a different split.

The rule: split at the level you want to generalise to.

you want to claim group by
works on a new read from a known strain nothing — random is fine
works on a new strain strain
works on a new species species
works on an uncultured environmental organism genus, or higher

In scikit-learn this is GroupKFold, StratifiedGroupKFold or LeaveOneGroupOut, passing groups=. It is one keyword argument, and it is the difference between a number you can report and a number you cannot.

Expect the score to drop

And let it

A grouped score is supposed to be lower than a random one. If it is not, either there is no structure in your data or the grouping does not capture it.

The temptation — to report the higher number because the lower one “looks bad” — is exactly how the leakage literature says published results get inflated.

Accuracy is the wrong headline

What to report instead

Accuracy hides everything that matters when classes are imbalanced, and taxonomic data is always imbalanced — reference databases have thousands of E. coli and one of most things.

A classifier that labels everything as the majority genus can score 95% accuracy and be useless. Report instead:

  • per-class recall — which genera does it systematically miss? The rare ones, invariably.
  • macro-averaged F1 — treats every class equally rather than weighting by frequency.
  • the confusion matrix — the only output that shows what it confuses with what. Confusions between closely related genera are expected and tolerable; confusions between distant ones mean something is broken.
  • accuracy at each rank — a classifier that is 60% right at genus may be 95% right at phylum. Reporting the rank you actually achieved is more useful than reporting the rank you wanted.

What a classifier should output

Three properties worth demanding

  1. A calibrated confidence. Not just a preferred class, but a number that means something — among the cases it reports at 0.9, roughly 90% should be right. Naive Bayes confidences are notoriously over-confident, because the independence assumption multiplies correlated evidence as though it were independent. Calibrate (CalibratedClassifierCV) before believing a probability.
  2. The ability to abstain. Below a confidence threshold, report nothing, or report at a coarser rank. A pipeline that must answer will invent answers — as this one did, at confidence 1.000.
  3. A record of what it was trained on. The only way anybody can judge whether your sample resembles the training distribution.

The RDP classifier and q2-feature-classifier do all three. A MultinomialNB you fitted yourself does none of them by default.

The trade-off

The two curves separate sharply. Below \(k \approx 5\) every possible \(k\)-mer is observed, so the features carry almost no positional information — all sequences look alike. Above \(k \approx 7\) most \(k\)-mers are seen once or never: the vector becomes mostly zeros, counts stop being estimates of anything, and the classifier is fitting noise.

Useful values for 16S work are typically 6–8 — large enough to be specific, small enough that counts are still counts. The right value depends on sequence length and how closely related your classes are, and is worth tuning rather than assuming.

The book itself uses \(k = 4\), chosen by experimenting on several Greengenes subsamples, and frames the same trade-off: longer \(k\)-mers are more taxon-specific, but less likely to be shared by close relatives at all.

Variations on the input

Where the book leaves the reader

  • Continuous labels — an organism’s abundance rather than its species — turn classification into regression: same feature table, different algorithms.
  • Features can be discrete too — the sex of a subject, a species present or absent.
  • scikit-learn ships other teaching datasets (diabetes, housing, handwritten digits) worth practising on.

If you can wrangle your data into a feature table, potentially with corresponding sample labels, you will likely be able to apply machine learning techniques to that data.

The book’s caveat follows straight after: knowing enough to run these methods is not knowing enough to run them correctly.

The thread through both lectures

Exact, then approximate

Lecture 1 built an algorithm that is provably optimal and quadratic. Lecture 2 threw the guarantee away to get three orders of magnitude, and then spent its time on the question that replaces it: how do we know the approximation is good enough?

Sensitivity, specificity, E-values and honest cross-validation are all answers to that one question.

That pattern — optimality abandoned, then the loss quantified — is the same one you saw in scheduling, and it is most of what applied computing consists of.
