Course projects

Seventeen data-science projects: nine geospatial, eight bioinformatic

The idea

Each project takes a domain — geospatial or biological — and asks for an analysis that a practitioner in that field would recognise as real work. But the point is not the domain. The point is that each domain breaks a different assumption that general data-science training quietly relies on, and learning to notice that is the transferable skill.

Every project therefore has a trap built into it: a plausible, textbook-correct analysis that gives a confidently wrong answer. The deliverable is not just the analysis — it is the demonstration that you found the trap and measured its size.

The thread running through the core eight

Standard machine learning assumes observations are independent and identically distributed. Neither domain obeys this, and they disobey it in structurally similar ways:

what is not independent consequence if ignored
Geospatial nearby places resemble each other (spatial autocorrelation) random CV leaks neighbours between folds; accuracy inflated
Bioinformatic related organisms, and cells from one donor random CV leaks near-duplicates; per-cell tests pseudoreplicate

Projects G2, G4, B2 and B4 are four views of this one problem. If students take away nothing else, it should be the reflex: before trusting a score, ask what the unit of independence actually is.

The further nine (G5–G9, B5–B8) turn on a second question, just as transferable: which of my results is a property of the data, and which is a property of a choice I made without examining it? — the study region, the class breaks, the distance metric, the alignment, the reference dataset.

Shared deliverable format

Regardless of project, submit:

  1. A rendered Quarto document — narrative, code, and figures together. Not a notebook dump; it should read as an argument.
  2. A reproducible environment — pyproject.toml/uv.lock and a single command that regenerates every figure from raw data.
  3. A “what I got wrong” section. Required, and graded. Describe the naive analysis, the number it produced, and the number the corrected analysis produced. A project with no such section is marked as incomplete, because it means the trap was never found.
  4. A sensitivity table. Pick the three parameters your conclusion depends on most and show how the conclusion moves across a defensible range of each.

Suggested weighting

component weight
correctness of the pipeline 30%
identifying and quantifying the trap 30%
sensitivity analysis and honest reporting of uncertainty 25%

Geospatial projects

Based on Geo 1–7: data models and CRS; geometric operations; spatial relationships and joins; rasters, xarray and Dask; spatial weights; choropleth classification; ESDA (Moran’s I, LISA, \(G_i^*\)); point patterns; spatial regression, feature engineering and spatial cross-validation.

Core projects

G1 — Accessibility, and the unit you chose to measure it in

Premise. Compute an accessibility index — how well-served each part of a city is by some amenity (clinics, schools, pharmacies, transit stops) — and then demonstrate that the ranking you produced is partly an artefact of your own spatial units.

Builds on. Geo 1 (CRS, vector data models), Geo 2 (centroids, buffers, dissolve, simplification), Geo 3 (spatial predicates, sjoin, grid aggregation).

Statistical toolkit. Aggregation and grouping.

  • Rank correlation — Spearman’s \(\rho\) and Kendall’s \(\tau\) to compare rankings across aggregations. Use rank rather than Pearson: you care whether the ordering is stable, not whether the values are.
  • The ecological fallacy — correlations computed on aggregates need not hold for individuals, and can even reverse sign. Robinson’s 1950 demonstration is the canonical one.
  • MAUP as scale effect plus zoning effect; the areal-unit case of the general problem that binning a continuous variable is a modelling decision.
  • Sensitivity analysis — vary the nuisance choice deliberately and report the spread as a result, not a footnote.

→ Aggregation and areal units

The task.

  1. Pull amenities for a city from OpenStreetMap with osmnx; obtain administrative boundaries and a population raster or table.
  2. Build a service-area layer (buffers, or isochrones if you go further) and join population to it.
  3. Produce a per-unit accessibility score and a choropleth ranking neighbourhoods.

The trap — the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. Repeat the whole analysis at three grid resolutions (say 250 m, 500 m, 1 km) and on two different administrative partitions. The rankings will disagree, sometimes dramatically. MAUP has two components and you should separate them:

  • scale effect — results change with the size of the units
  • zoning effect — results change with the shape of units at the same size

Report a rank-correlation matrix across all your aggregations. If the top-10 most under-served areas are not stable across units, say so plainly — that is the finding.

Second trap — CRS. Compute your buffers once in EPSG:4326 and once in an appropriate projected CRS. Report the area error. A “1 km buffer” in degrees is not 1 km, and the error varies with latitude.

Transferable technique. Aggregation-invariance testing; separating a real effect from an artefact of binning. The same problem appears any time you bin a continuous variable — the ecological fallacy is its non-spatial cousin.

Data. osmnx (already a dependency); national statistical office boundaries; WorldPop or a census table.

Difficulty. Moderate. Good first project — no new methods, but it forces genuine care with CRS and joins.

G2 — Hot spots, and how many of them are noise

Premise. Find statistically significant spatial clusters of some socioeconomic or epidemiological variable — then establish how many of those “significant” clusters you would have found in data with no structure at all.

Builds on. Geo 5 (spatial weights: rook, queen, KNN, kernel, block), Geo 6 (global Moran’s I, LISA, \(G_i^*\), conditional randomisation).

Statistical toolkit. Multiple testing under dependence.

  • Permutation / conditional randomisation tests — the reference distribution for LISA is built by shuffling values across units, so it is exact under the null and free of distributional assumptions.
  • FWER vs FDR — Bonferroni controls the probability of any false positive (conservative, and with 3 000 units it will leave almost nothing); Benjamini–Hochberg controls the expected proportion among rejections, which is usually the right target for exploratory mapping.
  • Benjamini–Yekutieli — the BH variant valid under arbitrary dependence. Spatial data is dependent by definition, so this is the honest choice, at the cost of a \(\log\) penalty.
  • The empirical null — permuting your variable across space and re-running the whole pipeline estimates the false-positive floor directly, without any correction theory.

→ Multiple testing

The task.

  1. Choose an areal dataset with a continuous variable (income, unemployment, disease incidence, election results).
  2. Global Moran’s I with a permutation test: is there autocorrelation at all?
  3. LISA cluster map: which areas are High-High, Low-Low, or spatial outliers?
  4. \(G_i^*\) hot-spot analysis as a cross-check.

The trap — LISA runs one hypothesis test per area. With 3 000 areas at \(\alpha = 0.05\), roughly 150 areas will be “significant” by chance. Required:

  • apply a multiple-comparison correction (Bonferroni, and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR) and report how many clusters survive each
  • run the whole LISA pipeline on a spatially randomised version of your variable — permute the values across areas — and count the “significant” clusters it finds. That count is your false-positive floor.

Second trap — the weights matrix is a modelling choice. Recompute the LISA map under rook, queen, KNN-4, KNN-8 and a distance-band \(W\). Produce a map showing, for each area, in how many of the five specifications it was significant. Areas significant under one specification only are not findings.

Transferable technique. Multiple testing under dependence; the fact that a graph you constructed determines the result. This is the same lesson as n_neighbors in Bio 3 and the same correction as pvals_adj in Bio 4.

Data. libpysal.examples ships several classic datasets; or national census data.

Difficulty. Moderate. The methods are all taught; the discipline is new.

G3 — Terrain at a scale that does not fit in memory

Premise. Build a landslide-susceptibility or solar-potential index from a digital elevation model large enough that you cannot load it, and show that the answer depends on the resolution you computed it at.

Builds on. Geo 4 (xarray/rioxarray, Dataset/DataArray, Dask chunking, dtype and memory management, clipping, zonal statistics), plus the Dask and DAG-scheduling material from the Big Data lectures.

Statistical toolkit. Scale, propagation and benchmarking.

  • Scale dependence of derived quantities — slope, curvature and TPI are defined relative to a cell size. Measured slope systematically flattens as resolution coarsens; it is not an estimate of one underlying value.
  • Error propagation — DEM vertical error propagates nonlinearly into slope and aspect; an index combining several derived layers compounds it.
  • Benchmarking methodology — report warm-up, repetitions and variance, not a single timing. Hoefler & Belli’s rules apply directly.
  • Complexity vs constants — \(O(n)\) per cell with a large constant can lose to \(O(n \log n)\) with a small one at realistic sizes.

→ Computation, scaling and benchmarking

The task.

  1. Acquire a DEM covering a region substantially larger than RAM (Copernicus GLO-30, SRTM, or a national DEM).
  2. Compute derived surfaces lazily: slope, aspect, hillshade, topographic position index.
  3. Combine into a susceptibility index; run zonal statistics per administrative unit; map the most exposed areas.
  4. Profile it: chunk sizes, memory high-water mark, task-graph size, wall time.

The trap — focal operations across chunk boundaries. Slope and TPI are window operations: each output pixel needs its neighbours. Compute them naively per chunk and every pixel on a chunk edge is wrong. Required: produce the buggy version, visualise the chunk-boundary artefacts (they appear as a grid of seams), then fix it with overlapping chunks (map_overlap / dask.array.overlap) and show the seams disappear.

Second trap — slope is scale-dependent. Compute slope at 30 m, 90 m and 500 m. Mean slope falls systematically as resolution coarsens; it is not a resolution-independent property of the terrain. Report how your susceptibility ranking changes, and state the resolution your conclusion is conditional on.

Transferable technique. Out-of-core computation, lazy evaluation, and the fact that partitioning data for parallelism changes results when operations are not element-wise. This connects directly to the work/span and DAG material in the Big Data lectures.

Difficulty. Hardest of the geo four. The chunk-boundary bug is genuinely instructive and genuinely annoying.

G4 — Predicting across space, and the accuracy you should not report

Premise. Train a model to predict a continuous outcome across space, then show that its cross-validated accuracy is substantially inflated, and report the honest number.

Builds on. All of Geo 1–7 — especially Geo 7, which supplies the whole of part 5 — plus the machine-learning knowledge students already have.

Statistical toolkit. Validation under dependence.

  • Optimism and generalisation error — the gap between training and test performance, and why resampling estimates it. ESL chapter 7 is the reference.
  • Blocked / grouped cross-validation — folds defined so that train and test are independent at the level you want to generalise to.
  • The variogram and its range — the distance at which spatial correlation dies out. It gives a principled starting block size instead of a guess.
  • Area of applicability — Meyer & Pebesma’s argument that a model should decline to predict where covariates fall outside its training distribution; the spatial cousin of B2’s abstention rule.
  • Residual diagnostics — Moran’s I on residuals tests whether the fitted model has absorbed the spatial structure.

→ Resampling and validation

The task.

  1. Choose a spatially continuous target — land surface temperature, air quality, property price, soil property — with covariates available as rasters or areal data.
  2. Assemble a feature table by sampling covariates at target locations (Geo 3/Geo 4).
  3. Fit any reasonable regressor. Evaluate with standard random \(k\)-fold CV.
  4. Re-evaluate with spatial block cross-validation: partition space into blocks and hold out whole blocks, so train and test points are never neighbours.
  5. Add a buffered/spatial leave-one-out variant that additionally excludes a radius around each test point.

The trap — spatial autocorrelation is leakage. Under random CV, a test point almost always has training points a few metres away, so the model interpolates rather than generalises. The R² can drop by a large margin under block CV — and the block-CV number is the one that predicts performance at a new location, which is the only thing anyone wants a spatial model for.

Required: report both numbers side by side, and plot performance against block size. As blocks grow, accuracy falls and then plateaus; where it plateaus tells you the range over which your model is really just interpolating.

Cross-check against the data itself: compute a variogram or Moran’s I of the residuals. Spatially autocorrelated residuals mean the model has not captured the spatial structure and your independence assumption is violated in the inference too.

Transferable technique. Blocked and grouped cross-validation. This is the same lesson as Bio 2’s GroupKFold — the rule in both is split at the level you want to generalise to. Students should be asked to state that connection explicitly.

Difficulty. Moderate methodologically, high conceptually. The best of the seventeen for demonstrating that a student has understood what validation is for.

Supported by Geo 7, which supplies the regression framework, the residual diagnostic, the feature engineering, and the spatial cross-validation this project turns on.

Further projects

These five use material the core four never touch — point patterns, choropleth classification, networks, long time series, and constrained clustering. Their common theme is not independence but the unexamined choice: each has a decision hidden inside it that determines the answer.

G5 — Is that a cluster, or is that what random looks like?

Premise. Decide whether a set of points is genuinely clustered, and discover that the answer depends on a boundary you drew yourself.

Builds on. Geo 6 (point pattern analysis: KDE, hexbinning, bounding shapes, DBSCAN, simulation envelopes), Geo 2 (convex and concave hulls), Geo 1 (CRS).

Statistical toolkit. Point processes.

  • Complete spatial randomness — the homogeneous Poisson process. “Clustered” means relative to CSR on a stated region, so the region is part of the hypothesis.
  • Ripley’s \(K\), and \(L\), \(G\), \(F\) — cumulative second-order summaries. \(K\) counts neighbours within distance \(r\); \(L\) is its variance-stabilised form; \(G\) and \(F\) are nearest-neighbour and empty-space distributions.
  • Monte Carlo envelopes — simulate the null many times and take pointwise quantiles. Note the multiple-comparison problem hiding here: a curve tested at many \(r\) simultaneously needs a global envelope, not pointwise.
  • Edge correction — Ripley’s isotropic or translation correction, since points near the boundary have artificially few neighbours.
  • Bandwidth selection for KDE — Silverman’s rule, cross-validated bandwidth, or adaptive; there is no correct value and the hotspot count depends on it.
  • Inhomogeneous Poisson — when intensity genuinely varies (population density), test against a fitted intensity rather than uniform CSR.

→ Point processes

The task.

  1. Take a point dataset — tokyo_clean.csv (10 000 geotagged photos), airports_clean, or crime/accident data for a city.
  2. Visualise density three ways: hexbin, KDE, and a quadrat count.
  3. Test against complete spatial randomness (CSR) using a simulation envelope on Ripley’s \(K\) or the \(G\) function.
  4. Cluster with DBSCAN and characterise the clusters.

The trap — the study region is an input, not a given. CSR means “uniform over the study area”, so the null hypothesis changes when the boundary does. Recompute the envelope under at least three regions: the minimum bounding rectangle, the convex hull, and an administrative boundary. Points near the edge have fewer neighbours simply because the region stops — that is the edge effect, and it biases \(K\) downward. Apply an edge correction and report the difference.

Second trap — the points are not independent observations. In tokyo_clean.csv there are 10 000 photos but only 1 825 users; one user contributed 419 photos, and the top 20 users account for 24.6% of the data. A tourist photographing one temple fifty times creates a “cluster” that is one person.

Required: re-run the whole analysis on one point per user (say, each user’s first photo) and compare. This is the point-pattern form of the pseudoreplication in B4, where one donor supplied four times as many cells as another.

Third trap — KDE bandwidth decides how many hotspots exist. Sweep it and show the count of apparent hotspots as a function of bandwidth. There is no correct value.

Transferable technique. Null models and what they assume; edge effects; deduplicating to the true unit of observation before testing.

Difficulty. Moderate. Conceptually the richest of the further four.

G6 — The same data, six maps

Premise. Take one variable, produce six defensible choropleths of it, and show that they support different conclusions. Then argue for one.

Builds on. Geo 6 (choropleth classification, mapclassify, ADCM, pooled classification), Geo 1 (CRS), Geo 2 (dissolve).

Statistical toolkit. Discretisation.

  • Classification as 1-D clustering — Fisher-Jenks is 1-D \(k\)-means; it minimises within-class variance optimally by dynamic programming.
  • Goodness of variance fit / ADCM — absolute deviation around class medians, measuring information lost to binning. Comparable across schemes with the same \(k\).
  • Quantiles and the appearance of structure — equal-count classes guarantee every class is populated, so even i.i.d. noise produces a “patterned” map. Demonstrating this on random data is the project’s core.
  • Heavy tails and head/tail breaks — Jiang’s scheme for variables where the mean splits a long tail meaningfully, which quantiles and equal-interval both mishandle.
  • Perceptual scaling — sequential vs diverging schemes encode different claims about the data; a diverging scheme asserts a meaningful midpoint.

→ Discretisation and visual encoding

The task.

  1. Choose an areal variable — brexit_vote, uscountypcincome, mexicojoin GDP.
  2. Map it under equal interval, quantiles, Fisher-Jenks, head-tail breaks, standard deviation, and a manual scheme of your own.
  3. Compute the absolute deviation around class medians (ADCM) for each, and rank the schemes by fit.
  4. Write the caption each map would carry in a newspaper.

The trap — classification is an argument, not a formatting step. Quantiles guarantee equal-sized classes and so always produce a map that looks patterned, even for random data. Prove it: generate a spatially random variable, map it with quantiles, and show it looks like a finding. Equal interval with a skewed variable puts almost everything in one class. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.

Second trap — comparing maps over time requires a pooled classification. With uscountypcincome (3 076 counties, 49 years, 1969–2017) or Mexican state GDP by decade, classify each year separately and then again with breaks pooled across all years. Separate classification makes every year look similar — the classes re-centre on that year’s distribution and hide the trend entirely. Only the pooled version shows change.

Third trap — colour. A sequential scheme implies order, a diverging scheme implies a meaningful midpoint. Choosing diverging with the midpoint at the mean invents a reference point that may not exist.

Transferable technique. Binning as a modelling decision; the difference between describing a distribution and describing a geography; honest visual communication.

Difficulty. Easy to start, hard to do well. The best project for students who think visualisation is the part after the analysis.

G7 — Distance is not distance

Premise. Rebuild G1’s accessibility index over the street network instead of Euclidean buffers, and quantify how wrong the buffers were.

Builds on. Geo 3 (spatial joins, nearest), Geo 5 (osmnx graphs), Geo 1 (CRS), Geo 7 (feature engineering), plus G1 if a student has done it.

Statistical toolkit. Graphs and structured error.

  • Shortest paths — Dijkstra and A*; weighted graphs where weight is travel time rather than length.
  • Structured vs random measurement error — Euclidean-vs-network error is not noise. It is systematic, spatially patterned, and correlated with the thing being measured, so it biases rather than merely inflating variance.
  • Non-random missingness — OSM footpath coverage varies with affluence, so missingness correlates with the outcome. This is MNAR, and no amount of data cleaning fixes it.
  • Change of support — network distance and Euclidean distance are different metrics over the same points; comparing indices built on each is a support problem.

→ Networks and structured error

The task.

  1. Download a city’s walking and driving networks with osmnx.
  2. Compute network-based service areas (isochrones) for a set of facilities.
  3. Compare, per unit, against the Euclidean buffer of the same radius: what fraction of the buffer is actually reachable?
  4. Recompute the accessibility ranking and compare against the Euclidean one.

The trap — Euclidean distance systematically overstates access, and not uniformly. Rivers, motorways, railways and cul-de-sacs all break the straight line. The error is worst exactly where it matters: a household across a motorway from a clinic is 200 m away and 2 km from its entrance.

Because the bias is spatially structured rather than random, it does not average out — it produces a map of error. Plot buffer area minus reachable area and show it has a geography.

Second trap — the mode is part of the question. A 15-minute walk and a 15-minute drive serve entirely different populations, and one-way systems mean drive-time is not even symmetric. State which mode your index is about; “accessibility” alone is under-specified.

Third trap — network completeness. OSM footpath coverage varies enormously between cities and neighbourhoods. A missing path looks exactly like an inaccessible area, and coverage tends to be worse in poorer areas — so the error correlates with the thing being studied.

Transferable technique. Choosing a distance metric that matches the process; graph algorithms on real infrastructure; recognising structured rather than random error.

Difficulty. Moderate. Pairs naturally with G1 — assign both to one student and the comparison is the deliverable.

G8 — Fifty years of inequality

Premise. Track income inequality across US counties from 1969 to 2017 and show that “is inequality rising?” has different answers depending on how you decompose it.

Builds on. Geo 7 part 1 (inequality indices, Theil decomposition, Moran’s I over time — worked on this dataset), Geo 6 (choropleth classification, Moran’s I, LISA), Geo 5 (spatial weights), Geo 2 (dissolve to regions).

Statistical toolkit. Inequality and distributional dynamics.

  • Inequality indices — Gini, coefficient of variation, and the Theil index. Each weights the distribution differently; Gini is most sensitive around the middle, Theil in the upper tail.
  • Additive decomposition — Theil (a generalised entropy measure) splits exactly into between-group and within-group components. Shorrocks proved the generalised entropy class is the only one that decomposes additively, which is why Gini does not.
  • \(\sigma\)- versus \(\beta\)-convergence — falling dispersion versus poor units growing faster. Quah’s “Galton’s fallacy” paper shows \(\beta\)-convergence can appear through regression to the mean with no convergence at all.
  • Rank mobility — transition matrices and Markov-chain models of distribution dynamics; measures movement rather than compression.
  • Index numbers — deflating a 49-year nominal series, and stating which price index you used.

→ Inequality and distributional dynamics

The task.

  1. Load uscountypcincome.gpkg — 3 076 counties, 49 annual per-capita income columns.
  2. Compute a national inequality index per year (Gini, Theil, coefficient of variation) and plot the trajectory.
  3. Decompose Theil into between-region and within-region components using census regions or states.
  4. Compute Moran’s I of income for each year and plot how spatial clustering evolves.
  5. Map the LISA clusters for the first and last year.

The trap — the decomposition depends entirely on the regions you chose. Split the same counties by state, by census region, and by a clustering of your own, and the between/within split changes substantially. “Inequality is mostly between regions” is a statement about your regionalisation as much as about the economy — MAUP again, now in a statistic rather than a map.

Second trap — convergence in levels and in ranks are different questions. Counties can converge in income while the ranking stays frozen, which means nobody has actually moved. Compute both: \(\sigma\)-convergence (dispersion falling over time) and rank mobility between 1969 and 2017. They can point opposite ways, and the disagreement is the interesting result.

Third trap — nominal income over 49 years. Deflate it, and state which index you used. An undeflated trend measures mostly inflation.

Transferable technique. Index decomposition and its sensitivity to grouping; distinguishing change in distribution from change in composition; long-series hygiene.

Difficulty. Moderate. The data is bundled, clean and genuinely longitudinal, which is rare in a teaching dataset.

Note

Geo 7 part 1 now teaches this material directly — Gini and Theil, the additive decomposition, and Moran’s I across all 49 years, worked on this exact dataset. Students taking G8 should start there. The deck also demonstrates the trap: over 1969–2017 Gini falls while Theil rises, and the between/within decomposition explains why.

G9 — Drawing boundaries that have to work

Premise. Build a set of regions from data — service districts, catchment areas, electoral districts — and confront the fact that “good regions” has several definitions that cannot be satisfied at once.

Builds on. Geo 7 part 2 (geodemographic clustering, contiguity-constrained regionalization, geographic vs feature coherence, solution similarity), Geo 5 (spatial weights), Geo 2 (dissolve, hulls, compactness geometry), Geo 6 (mapping the result).

Statistical toolkit. Constrained clustering and multi-objective choice.

  • Clustering under a hard constraint — contiguity as a connectivity graph, and size/threshold constraints as in the max-\(p\)-regions problem, where the number of regions is an output rather than an input.
  • Evaluation without ground truth — silhouette and within-cluster variance for feature coherence; ARI across seeds and algorithms for stability. There is no correct answer to compare against, which is the defining difficulty.
  • Compactness measures — Polsby-Popper (\(4\pi A / P^2\)), Reock (area over minimum bounding circle), and convex-hull ratio. Each is a different formalisation of one intuition.
  • Multi-objective optimisation — geographic coherence, feature coherence and size balance form a Pareto front. Reporting one solution hides the trade-off; reporting the front is the honest output.
  • Ensemble methods — sampling many valid partitions (Markov-chain approaches such as ReCom) to ask whether an observed plan is unusual among the plans that were possible.

→ Distance-based multivariate analysis and Inequality and distributional dynamics

The task.

  1. Take sandiego_tracts.gpkg (628 tracts, bundled and used in Geo 7) or census geography for a region of your choice.
  2. Cluster without a spatial constraint (k-means, Ward) and count the disconnected fragments each “cluster” forms.
  3. Regionalize with contiguity, and measure what the constraint cost in feature coherence.
  4. Add a size constraint — every region must contain at least \(n\) people — using spopt.region.MaxPHeuristic. Note that the number of regions is now determined by the threshold, not chosen by you.
  5. Evaluate every solution on all three criteria and present the trade-off, not a winner.

The trap — “make them compact” is not a well-defined instruction. Compute Polsby-Popper, Reock and the convex-hull ratio for the same regions and compare the rankings. On the verified San Diego solution they disagree substantially: rank correlation between Polsby-Popper and Reock is only Spearman 0.56, and the raw ranges barely overlap (PP 0.05–0.14, Reock 0.18–0.51).

So a regionalisation that is “most compact” under one measure need not be under another. Required: state which measure you optimised, show the ranking under at least two others, and say whether your conclusion survives.

Second trap — contiguous, compact and equal-sized are jointly infeasible. This is the central result of the redistricting literature, not a limitation of your code. Verified here: MaxPHeuristic with a threshold of 273 638 people yields 11 regions ranging 273 898 to 344 882 — a 1.26× spread while satisfying the minimum. Tighten the threshold and either contiguity or compactness degrades.

Required: sweep the threshold and plot the achievable frontier of size-balance against compactness. That plot is the deliverable, not any single map.

Third trap — the number of regions is not in the data. With a contiguity constraint the usual elbow heuristics stop working, because the objective changes monotonically in a way that gives no clean kink. Under max-\(p\) the count is an output of the threshold you chose — so the apparently technical question “how many regions?” is answered by a policy decision about minimum viable size.

And the reason this matters. These are the mechanics of gerrymandering. Every criterion here — contiguity, compactness, equal population, communities of interest — appears in redistricting law, and the fact that they conflict is precisely what makes districting contestable. The modern response is not a better single plan but an ensemble: generate many valid plans and ask where the proposed one sits among them. Students who take this project should read the ReCom paper and understand why “we drew a compact, contiguous, equal-population map” is not a defence.

Data. sandiego_tracts.gpkg (bundled, includes total_pop); or uscountypcincome.gpkg; or census geography for a city you care about.

Difficulty. Moderate to hard. Conceptually the most open-ended of the seventeen, and the one with the clearest line to public policy.

Bioinformatics projects

Core projects

B1 — Build a homology searcher, then find out what it cannot see

Premise. Implement seed-and-extend search, benchmark it against exhaustive Smith-Waterman, and characterise the detection limit — not as one number, but as a function of evolutionary distance.

Builds on. Bio 1 (Needleman-Wunsch, Smith-Waterman, substitution matrices, affine gaps, \(O(mn)\)), Bio 2 (\(k\)-mer indexing, minimizers, E-values, sensitivity/specificity).

Statistical toolkit. Evaluating a retrieval system.

  • ROC and precision-recall — AUC summarises ranking quality independent of threshold. Under heavy class imbalance (a few homologs among many decoys) PR curves are more informative than ROC, which Saito & Rehmsmeier demonstrate directly.
  • Stratified evaluation — reporting one aggregate over a heterogeneous benchmark hides exactly the failure you care about. Stratify by percent identity.
  • Extreme value theory — alignment scores of unrelated sequences follow a Gumbel distribution; the E-value is a tail probability from it. This is why E-values scale with database size.
  • Speed–accuracy frontiers — plotting the achievable set rather than reporting one operating point.

→ Classification and evaluation

Read first. Reading notes: the DP recurrence, four modes, what it costs, why \(k\)-mer filtering works, what the heuristic gives up, profiles and the twilight zone.

The task.

  1. Build a labelled benchmark: true homologs at controlled divergence plus decoys. Use real sequences (a Pfam family, or orthologue sets) rather than only synthetic ones.
  2. Implement an inverted \(k\)-mer index with seed-and-extend, and a minimizer variant.
  3. Evaluate against exhaustive SW as ground truth: ROC and precision-recall, plus wall-time and index memory.
  4. Sweep word size \(w\) and minimizer window, and map the speed–sensitivity frontier.

The trap — aggregate sensitivity hides the failure. A searcher can report 95% overall sensitivity while finding none of the remote homologs, simply because most of the benchmark is easy. Required: report sensitivity stratified by percent identity, and identify the divergence at which detection collapses into the noise band. Relate it to the twilight zone.

Second trap — search in the right alphabet. Repeat the detection-limit experiment on translated protein sequences. Homology stays visible substantially further out, because synonymous substitutions do not change the protein. Quantify the difference.

Transferable technique. Stratified evaluation; speed–accuracy frontiers; the discipline of never reporting a single aggregate metric for a heterogeneous benchmark. Directly applicable to any retrieval or approximate-nearest-neighbour system.

Difficulty. Moderate. Mostly implementation; the evaluation design is the interesting part.

B2 — A taxonomic classifier that knows when to say “I don’t know”

Premise. Build a 16S taxonomic classifier, and make it refuse to answer when it should — then show that the refusal mechanism is what makes it usable.

Builds on. Bio 2 (\(k\)-mer features, Naive Bayes, cross-validation, leakage, bootstrap confidence, abstention, accuracy-versus-coverage).

Statistical toolkit. Classification with a reject option.

  • Naive Bayes — conditional independence, MAP estimation, and Laplace/Lidstone smoothing for unseen \(k\)-mers.
  • Grouped cross-validation — GroupKFold/StratifiedGroupKFold, and the general principle from Roberts et al. and Kapoor & Narayanan.
  • Calibration — reliability diagrams, expected calibration error, and post-hoc fixes (Platt scaling, isotonic regression). Naive Bayes is badly calibrated by construction because it multiplies correlated evidence.
  • Rejection learning — Chow’s 1970 error/reject trade-off is the original formal treatment; the accuracy-versus-coverage curve is its modern presentation.
  • Bootstrap — resampling the query’s own features to estimate stability, which is what RDP confidence actually is.

→ Classification and evaluation

Read first. Reading notes: the feature table, Naive Bayes over \(k\)-mers, the leakage trap, what leakage actually costs, the confidence measure that does work, the fix, three things to demand.

The task.

  1. Obtain a real reference set with taxonomy (SILVA, Greengenes2, or RDP).
  2. Build \(k\)-mer features; tune \(k\); train a classifier.
  3. Evaluate three ways: random \(k\)-fold CV; grouped CV holding out whole species; and grouped CV holding out whole genera.
  4. Implement RDP-style bootstrap confidence and an abstention rule.
  5. Test on organisms from clades entirely absent from training.

The trap — reference databases are phylogenetically structured, not i.i.d. Random CV will report near-perfect accuracy. The species-level and genus-level grouped scores will be progressively and substantially worse, and the genus-level score is the one that predicts performance on an environmental sample.

Required deliverable: an accuracy-versus-coverage curve. Sweep the confidence threshold; at each point plot the fraction of queries answered against the accuracy of those answers. A classifier that answers 60% of queries at 95% accuracy is more useful than one that answers 100% at 70%, and the curve is what lets you argue that.

Second trap — report at the rank you achieved. Accuracy at genus may be poor while accuracy at phylum is excellent. Back off rather than guess.

Transferable technique. Grouped cross-validation, calibration, rejection learning, and accuracy–coverage tradeoffs. This is exactly the structure of any production classifier that must handle out-of-distribution input.

Difficulty. Moderate. The conceptual core is already taught; the work is real data and careful evaluation.

B3 — A single-cell analysis you can defend

Premise. Run a complete scRNA-seq pipeline on a real dataset and produce an annotated cell atlas — then demonstrate which of your conclusions survive the parameter choices you made along the way.

Builds on. Bio 3 (QC and MAD outliers, ambient RNA, doublets, normalization, feature selection, PCA, neighbours, UMAP) and Bio 4 (Leiden, resolution, markers, annotation, sub-clustering, stability).

Statistical toolkit. Pipelines and their sensitivity.

  • Variance-stabilising transforms — the Gamma-Poisson (negative binomial) mean-variance relationship, why \(\log(x+1)\) partially stabilises it, and Pearson residuals as the alternative.
  • Robust statistics — the MAD as a breakdown-resistant spread estimate; why outlier detection built on \(\sigma\) hides the very outliers it should find.
  • PCA — and its assumption of approximately homoscedastic, roughly linear structure, which count data violates before transformation.
  • Cluster comparison — the adjusted Rand index (Hubert & Arabie), chance-corrected so that two random partitions score ≈ 0.
  • Selective inference — clustering and then testing on the same data invalidates the \(p\)-values; the markers are a ranking, not a test.

→ Single-cell statistics

Read first. Reading notes: all of Part 3, then clustering, the resolution problem, sub-clustering, annotation, a worked failure.

The task.

  1. Choose a real public dataset other than PBMC3k — sc.datasets.paul15 (mouse haematopoiesis), pbmc68k_reduced, a CELLxGENE download, or a GEO accession.
  2. Full pipeline through to annotated clusters with marker evidence.
  3. Sub-cluster at least one population, recomputing HVGs and PCA within it.
  4. Annotate manually from markers, and defend every label.

The trap — every default is a modelling choice. Required: a factorial sensitivity analysis over at least

  • 2 normalizations (shifted log, analytic Pearson residuals)
  • 2 HVG counts (2 000, 4 000)
  • 3 Leiden resolutions

For each combination report the cluster count and the adjusted Rand index against your reference partition. Then state explicitly: which biological conclusions are stable across all twelve runs, and which appear only under some settings? The unstable ones are not findings.

Second trap — double dipping. Your marker \(p\)-values are computed on the same data that defined the clusters. Report the markers as a ranking, state why the \(p\)-values are inflated, and do not use them as evidence that a cluster is real. Use stability and marker interpretability for that instead.

Transferable technique. Pipeline sensitivity analysis and the distinction between a result and an artefact of hyperparameters. Applies to every multi-stage pipeline anyone will ever build.

Difficulty. Moderate. Long but well-supported by the lectures. Computationally the heaviest of the bio four.

B4 — Did anything actually change?

Premise. Given cells from multiple samples in two conditions, determine what differs — and quantify how badly the obvious analysis overstates it.

Builds on. Bio 4 (batch effects, integration, Harmony, pseudobulk, pseudoreplication, compositional analysis, multiple testing, power).

Statistical toolkit. Nested data.

  • Pseudoreplication — Hurlbert’s 1984 paper named it for ecology; it is the same error, and single-cell reinvented it forty years later.
  • Aggregation vs mixed models — pseudobulk sums to the true replicate level; a mixed model with a random donor effect keeps cell-level resolution while modelling the dependence. Both are defensible; per-cell Wilcoxon is not.
  • Negative binomial GLMs with empirical-Bayes shrinkage — DESeq2 and edgeR share dispersion information across genes, which is why their rankings reproduce better than per-gene estimates.
  • Compositional data — Aitchison’s log-ratio framework. Proportions live on a simplex, so ordinary tests on them are invalid; CLR moves them to real space.
  • FDR and power — Benjamini–Hochberg, and the arithmetic of how few replicates support how large an effect.
  • Negative controls — constructing a comparison where the truth is known null is the cheapest validity check in applied statistics.

→ Nested data and differential testing

Read first. Reading notes: integration, deep generative integration, conditions and why per-cell tests are wrong, multiple testing.

The task.

  1. Load sc.datasets.ebi_expression_atlas("E-GEOD-150728") — 57 096 PBMCs from 6 COVID-19 patients and 6 healthy donors. (Alternatives and one deliberately underpowered dataset are listed under Extensions needed.)
  2. Integrate across samples; verify integration did not erase condition-specific populations.
  3. Annotate cell types consistently across conditions.
  4. Test for condition effects two ways: naive per-cell Wilcoxon, and pseudobulk with a proper bulk method.
  5. Run a compositional analysis of cell-type proportions.

The trap — pseudoreplication. The per-cell test treats 10 000 cells as 10 000 independent observations when there are perhaps 6 donors. Required: report the number of “significant” genes from each approach and the ratio between them. It is typically an order of magnitude or more. Then run a negative control: split the control samples into two fake groups and test for differences between them. The per-cell test will find hundreds of significant genes where no difference exists; pseudobulk should find approximately none. That comparison is the deliverable.

Second trap — compositional closure. Proportions sum to one, so one expanding cell type forces every other to appear depleted. Test naively per cell type, then with a centred-log-ratio transform or scCODA, and compare the conclusions.

Third trap — unequal cells per donor. In E-GEOD-150728 donors contribute between 2 316 and 10 503 cells. A per-cell test therefore weights donor C1 more than four times as heavily as C2, so part of what it calls a “COVID effect” is a C1 effect. Show this directly: drop the largest donor and re-run the per-cell test. If the gene list moves substantially, the original result was partly one person. Pseudobulk is immune to this by construction, which is a second independent argument for it.

Transferable technique. Hierarchical and nested data; aggregation as a defence against pseudoreplication; compositional data analysis. The negative-control design — inventing a comparison where the truth is known to be null — is one of the most broadly useful habits in applied statistics.

Difficulty. Hardest of the core bio projects, and the most valuable. Pseudoreplication is the single most common error in published single-cell work.

B5 — A microbiome community analysis

Premise. Compare microbial communities across samples, and discover that the conclusion turns on the distance metric and the normalisation, neither of which has a correct answer.

Builds on. Bio 2 (ordination, PCoA, Jaccard distance, beta_diversity, reading an ordination plot), Bio 4 (compositional analysis, multiple testing), Bio 1 (\(k\)-mers).

Statistical toolkit. Distance-based multivariate analysis.

  • Beta-diversity metrics — Jaccard (presence/absence), Bray-Curtis (abundance), and the fact that they weight rare taxa completely differently.
  • PCoA / classical MDS — eigendecomposition of a centred distance matrix; equivalent to PCA when the distance is Euclidean, and a way to ordinate any distance when it is not.
  • PERMANOVA — Anderson’s permutational MANOVA: partitions a distance matrix by group and tests with permutations. Note it is sensitive to dispersion differences as well as location, so a significant result may mean “one group is more variable”, not “the groups differ” — check with a dispersion test.
  • Compositional data analysis — Gloor et al. argue microbiome data is compositional and that ignoring it is not optional.
  • Rarefaction — and McMurdie & Holmes’ argument that it is statistically inadmissible, against the practical fact that richness scales with depth.

→ Distance-based multivariate analysis

The task.

  1. Build a feature table from 16S sequences — qiime_default_reference gives you 99 322 labelled sequences to construct realistic synthetic communities, or use a real study’s table.
  2. Compute beta diversity under several metrics: Jaccard (presence/absence), Bray-Curtis (abundance), and a phylogeny-aware one if available.
  3. Ordinate with PCoA and plot by group.
  4. Test group separation formally with PERMANOVA (skbio.stats.distance.permanova).
  5. Identify differentially abundant taxa.

The trap — compositional data. Sequencing returns relative abundances: the total is fixed by how much you sequenced, not by how much was there. So one taxon blooming forces every other to appear to decline. This is the same closure problem as B4’s cell-type proportions, and here it is unavoidable rather than incidental.

Required: analyse the same data both naively (proportions) and with a centred log-ratio transform, and compare which taxa are called differential.

Second trap — presence/absence versus abundance changes the answer. Jaccard weights a rare taxon exactly as much as a dominant one; Bray-Curtis nearly ignores rare taxa. Run both, plot both ordinations, and report whether your group separation survives the choice. If it does not, the finding is about the metric.

Third trap — rarefaction. Samples differ in sequencing depth, and deeper samples detect more taxa purely for that reason. Subsampling to even depth throws away data; not doing it confounds richness with effort. Show the relationship between depth and observed richness in your data, then defend whichever you chose.

Transferable technique. Compositional data analysis; distance-metric sensitivity; PERMANOVA as a distance-based alternative to per-feature testing.

Difficulty. Moderate. The closest thing here to a classical ecology analysis.

B6 — From alignment to tree, and how much to believe it

Premise. Build a multiple sequence alignment, infer a phylogeny from it, and demonstrate that the tree is less certain than any single figure suggests.

Builds on. Bio 1 (pairwise alignment, MSA, progressive alignment, “once a gap, always a gap”, scoring matrices), Bio 2 (profiles, the twilight zone).

Statistical toolkit. Resampling a dependent structure.

  • Distance-based tree building — UPGMA (assumes a molecular clock) versus neighbour-joining (does not). The assumption matters more than the software.
  • The phylogenetic bootstrap — Felsenstein 1985: resample alignment columns, not sequences. Columns are the independent-ish units; sequences are the thing being estimated.
  • What bootstrap support means — Efron, Halloran & Holmes examine its interpretation. It measures consistency of the data you have, so systematic error is invisible to it: a wrong clade from a bad alignment gets 100% support.
  • Tree distances — Robinson-Foulds for comparing topologies, so “the trees disagree” becomes a number.
  • Sampling vs systematic error — the general distinction this project is built to teach.

→ Resampling and validation

The task.

  1. Assemble a protein or gene family — a Pfam seed alignment, or orthologues you collect.
  2. Align it. Do this more than once: with different tools or different parameters.
  3. Build distance-based trees with Bio.Phylo’s DistanceCalculator and DistanceTreeConstructor.
  4. Bootstrap: resample alignment columns with replacement, rebuild, and compute support for each clade.
  5. Report the consensus tree with support values.

The trap — alignment uncertainty never reaches the tree. Standard practice builds one alignment, treats it as observed data, and infers a tree from it. But the alignment is an estimate, produced by a greedy algorithm whose guide tree came from the very distances you are about to re-estimate.

Required: build trees from two or three different alignments of the same sequences and compare topologies (Robinson-Foulds distance, or just inspect the clades). Where they disagree, no amount of bootstrapping on a single alignment would have told you.

Second trap — bootstrap support is not probability of correctness. It measures how consistently the columns you have support a clade. Systematic error — a bad alignment, a wrong model — is reproduced identically in every bootstrap replicate, so a wrong clade can have 100% support.

Third trap — trimming changes the tree. Gappy columns are the least reliable and also the most variable. Run with and without trimAl-style column filtering and compare.

Transferable technique. Propagating uncertainty through a multi-stage pipeline; distinguishing sampling error from systematic error; resampling the right unit (columns, not sequences).

Difficulty. Moderate to hard. The most “classical bioinformatics” project here, and the one that best exercises Bio 1’s MSA section.

B7 — Making a single-cell pipeline scale

Premise. Take a working pipeline and make it handle ten times the data, measuring every step — and show that the obvious optimisations are the wrong ones.

Builds on. Bio 3 (sparse formats CSR/CSC/COO, AnnData backed mode, memory arithmetic, where the time goes), plus the Dask and DAG-scheduling material from the Big Data lectures. The bioinformatics counterpart of G3.

Statistical toolkit. Measurement, not inference.

  • Benchmarking methodology — Hoefler & Belli’s rules: report the distribution not the minimum, state warm-up and repetitions, and never compare single runs.
  • Complexity analysis — where each pipeline step sits (\(O(n)\) in stored values for normalisation, roughly \(O(n \log n)\) for approximate kNN) and why the asymptotically expensive step is the one to attack.
  • Sparse linear algebra — CSR/CSC/COO and why access pattern, not size, decides the cost.
  • Approximate nearest neighbours — the recall/speed trade-off, and that lowering recall is a change of method, not an optimisation.
  • Equivalence testing — ARI against the unoptimised run, to prove the answer did not move.

→ Computation, scaling and benchmarking

The task.

  1. Start from B3’s pipeline, or the Bio 3 deck’s.
  2. Profile it properly — wall time and peak memory per step, not guesses.
  3. Scale the input: 2 700 cells → 20 000 → 100 000+ (concatenate atlas datasets, or subsample a large one).
  4. Measure how each step scales, and identify the actual bottleneck.
  5. Optimise it, and prove the results are unchanged (identical clusters, ARI = 1.0).

The trap — the bottleneck is not where you think. Most students optimise normalisation, which is linear in stored values and trivially fast. The real cost is almost always the neighbour graph — approximate \(k\)-NN over \(n\) cells in 50 dimensions — followed by UMAP. Profile first; the whole project is an argument for profiling before optimising.

Second trap — the format has to match the access pattern. A per-gene loop over a CSR matrix is dramatically slower than the same loop over CSC — about 55× on PBMC3k — and nothing warns you. Demonstrate the cost directly, and show the CSR/CSC/COO/dense memory comparison at your largest scale.

Third trap — an optimisation that changes the answer is not an optimisation. Lower n_pcs, fewer neighbours or a coarser approximate-kNN all speed things up by doing less work. Any of those is legitimate only if reported as a change in method. Verify cluster identity with ARI against the unoptimised run, and state the speed-up alongside it.

Transferable technique. Profiling, sparse data structures, out-of-core and lazy evaluation, and holding correctness fixed while changing performance.

Difficulty. Moderate. The most engineering-flavoured project of the seventeen, and the best preparation for real work.

B8 — A classifier that meets a tissue it has never seen

Premise. Train a cell-type classifier on one dataset, apply it to another, and measure what it does with cell types that were not in the reference.

Builds on. Bio 4 (annotation, automated annotation, uncertainty, integration), Bio 2 (classification, grouped CV, abstention, bootstrap confidence). It is B2’s lesson in single-cell clothing.

Statistical toolkit. Distribution shift.

  • Covariate shift and domain adaptation — \(P(X)\) differs between reference and query while \(P(Y \mid X)\) is assumed stable. Batch effect is covariate shift with a biological name.
  • Out-of-distribution detection — distinguishing “I am unsure between these classes” from “this is unlike anything I know”. A softmax cannot express the second; distance to the training manifold can.
  • Rejection learning — the same accuracy-versus-coverage machinery as B2, now on cells.
  • Calibration under shift — a classifier calibrated on the reference is generally not calibrated on the query, which is why reported confidence degrades faster than accuracy.
  • Label taxonomy harmonisation — evaluating at several granularities rather than forcing a one-to-one mapping between annotation schemes.

→ Transfer, shift and out-of-distribution

The task.

  1. Take two annotated datasets — PBMC3k plus one of the EBI accessions from B4 (E-CURD-112 carries author and ontology cell-type labels; E-HCAD-31 carries ontology labels only).
  2. Train a classifier on the reference’s expression and labels.
  3. Predict on the query, and compare against its own labels where they exist.
  4. Add an abstention rule and produce an accuracy-versus-coverage curve.
  5. Deliberately hold out one cell type from training, and examine what the classifier does with it.

The trap — the reference is never complete. A held-out cell type does not come back as “unknown”. It is confidently assigned to whatever it most resembles, exactly as the novel genus in Bio 2 was assigned to Genus_C at confidence 1.000. Quantify it: what fraction of held-out-type cells fall below your abstention threshold, and what fraction sail through?

Second trap — batch effect masquerades as biology. Reference and query differ by protocol, donor and lab as well as by cell type. Without integration the classifier may be recognising the dataset, not the cell type. Test it: train on reference, predict on reference cells held out — high accuracy. Then predict on the query — and attribute the drop between batch effect and genuine difficulty.

Third trap — label granularity does not match. One dataset says “T cell”, the other says “CD4 naive”, “CD4 memory”, “CD8 effector”. There is no correct mapping, and harmonising is a modelling decision. Report accuracy at each level of granularity rather than forcing one.

Transferable technique. Transfer learning and domain shift; out-of-distribution detection; reconciling label taxonomies between datasets.

Difficulty. Hard. The most realistic of the bio projects — reference mapping is what most working single-cell analysts actually do.

The part the book does not have

Cross-validation appears nowhere in Part III — I checked all four chapters for cross-valid, cross_val, KFold, train_test, holdout and overfit and found zero hits. gdsbook fits spatial models and interprets coefficients; it never evaluates out-of-sample prediction.

That section was therefore written from scratch, attached where it follows naturally — straight after chapter 11’s own finding that residuals are spatially autocorrelated (measured in the deck at \(\text{corr}(u, Wu) = 0.33\) for \(k=20\)). It covers why autocorrelated residuals make random folds leak, block CV via GroupKFold, the block-size sweep, buffered leave-one-out, and closing the loop with Moran’s I on residuals.

Its centrepiece is a three-model comparison that isolates the leak:

model random CV block CV
coordinates only 0.166 −0.103
house features only 0.607 0.583
features + coordinates 0.653 0.602

A model using nothing but latitude and longitude looks like it has learned something under random CV and scores worse than the mean under block CV. All of its apparent skill was memorising neighbours. House features, which are not spatial, barely move. That contrast is the lesson, and it transfers directly to B2’s grouped CV.

B2 data — solved, and from readIAB’s own source

readIAB chapter 5 builds its 16S feature table from qiime_default_reference, a pip package shipping the Greengenes reference with matching taxonomy. It is now a declared dependency. Verified contents:

qiime_default_reference.get_reference_sequences()  -> 97_otus.fasta        143.2 MB
qiime_default_reference.get_reference_taxonomy()   -> 97_otu_taxonomy.txt   10.1 MB

99,322 sequences   1,816 distinct genera   7-rank taxonomy strings

This is ideal for B2 and needs no data preparation or zip:

  • Real, labelled, and large enough to subsample honestly at several taxonomic ranks.
  • Grouped CV falls out of the taxonomy string. Each record is k__…;p__…;c__…;o__…;f__…;g__…;s__…, so the grouping key for species-level or genus-level held-out evaluation is a split(';') away. No extra metadata to source.
  • It contains its own unknowns. 63,887 of the 99,322 records have an unassigned genus (g__ with nothing after it). Those are not a defect — they are exactly the “reference databases are incomplete” case the project is about, available for free.
  • Same source as the lecture, so students are working with the data the textbook used.

Install it with uv sync; the 153 MB ships inside the wheel, so there is no download step at analysis time.

B4 data — EBI Single Cell Expression Atlas, no new dependency

scanpy ships no multi-condition dataset, but it ships a fetcher for one of the largest curated collections of them:

adata = sc.datasets.ebi_expression_atlas("E-GEOD-150728")

The returned AnnData carries the experiment’s design in .obs as Sample Characteristic[...] and Factor Value[...] columns — donor, condition, sex, age. That is exactly the metadata B4 needs, with nothing to prepare.

The atlas has no programmatic filter for design shape, so I searched its 384 experiments and checked donor counts per condition on the promising ones. Four are suitable, and they differ in a way you can exploit:

accession cells design cell-type labels use
E-GEOD-150728 57 096 6 COVID-19 vs 6 healthy no primary
E-CURD-112 34 015 5 healthy vs 4 Down syndrome yes alternative
E-HCAD-31 18 694 6 healthy vs 3 type-2 diabetes yes alternative, unbalanced
E-HCAD-11 21 664 2 healthy vs 2 ulcerative colitis yes the cautionary one

Primary: E-GEOD-150728 (Wilk et al. 2020, COVID-19 PBMC). Verified: 57 096 cells × 34 404 genes, donors C1–C7 and H1–H6, perfectly balanced 6-versus-6.

Why it is the right choice:

  • 6 per group is exactly the floor Bio 4 names for a defensible DE analysis — enough that pseudobulk genuinely has power, so the naive-versus-pseudobulk contrast is informative rather than just “no power either way”.
  • PBMCs, so students annotate using the marker biology they already met in Bio 3 and Bio 4 on PBMC3k.
  • Cells per donor range from 2 316 to 10 503. This is a gift: a per-cell test is additionally dominated by whichever donors contributed most cells, so the naive analysis is partly measuring donor C1. Ask students to show that.
  • It carries no author cell-type labels, so the annotation work is real. Students wanting a reference to check against should use E-CURD-112 or E-HCAD-31, which do.

Assign E-HCAD-11 to at least one student as a contrast. At 2 donors per group it is underpowered by construction, and the correct conclusion is “this design cannot support the claim” — which is precisely the lesson of the “How many samples?” slide, and a more valuable answer than a gene list. Comparing that report against a 6-versus-6 report makes the point better than either alone.
